# Hotel Business Models, Profitability, ROI and Upselling

Research date: 17 September 2026  
Project: CDE4301  
Scope: Business context for problem discovery; not a proposal for a particular solution or an investment recommendation.

## 1. Executive Summary

Rooms are generally the main revenue engine of a conventional hotel. F&B is an important secondary business, while events can generate several revenue streams simultaneously. A profitable department does not automatically imply a high return on the money invested in the property.

Upselling is an established hotel practice and can increase contribution from an existing booking. However, public evidence does not establish a universal adoption rate, conversion rate or share of hotel profit attributable to upselling.

For this project, the important question is not merely whether staff can sell more. It is whether operational pressures prevent them from recognising suitable opportunities and delivering what has already been promised.

## 2. Revenue, Profit and ROI Are Different

| Measure | Meaning | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | Sales before expenses | Whether the activity makes money |
| Departmental contribution | Department revenue less the expenses assigned to that department | Profit after shared overhead or property investment |
| Departmental margin | Departmental contribution / department revenue | Investor return |
| Hotel operating profit or GOP | Operating surplus under a specified accounting definition | Cash available after every ownership cost |
| Owner cash flow | Cash remaining after the specified operating, financing, tax and capital obligations | Total investment return including eventual sale |
| Investment return | Cash distributions and value changes relative to invested capital, over a stated period | A universal percentage for all hotels |

Always check definitions: sources differ in whether management fees sit above or below a reported operating-profit measure.

For a simple annual cash yield, divide annual distributable cash by invested equity. For a multi-year ownership investment, analyse dated cash flows and sale proceeds using IRR/NPV. A capitalisation rate, an operating margin and an equity IRR are not interchangeable.

**Illustration, not market data:** an owner invests S$100 million of equity and receives S$5 million annual cash after the stated expenses. That is a 5% annual cash yield, even if the rooms department has a much higher operating margin. It excludes any later capital gain or loss.

A credible ROI estimate requires purchase/development cost, financing, renovation spending, operating forecasts, holding period and exit assumptions. The visit notes do not contain these inputs.

## 3. Which Activities Generate the Most Money?

### 3.1 Financial example: rooms versus F&B

Host Hotels & Resorts' 2025 consolidated results provide a predominantly US luxury/upper-upscale ownership example. Amounts below are US$ million; calculations are ours.

| Measure | Rooms | F&B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 3,608 | 1,803 |
| Share of hotel revenue excluding condominium sales | 60.0% | 30.0% |
| Separately reported departmental expenses | 906 | 1,224 |
| Revenue less those expenses | 2,702 | 579 |
| Resulting departmental margin | 74.9% | 32.1% |

Denominator: 3,608 + 1,803 + 604 other revenue = 6,015; excludes 99 condominium sales. Shared support, management, property expenses, depreciation and financing are not deducted from these departmental margins. This is one portfolio, not a Singapore benchmark. [Host, 2025 results](https://www.hosthotels.com/Press-Releases/2026/PressRelease_27966)

### 3.2 Broader F&B evidence

CBRE identifies rooms as the largest hotel revenue source and F&B historically second. Its monthly sample of 866 US properties recorded a 29.1% F&B departmental margin in January–June 2025. This is not a restaurant net margin or investment ROI. Public-room rental and service charges are included in its F&B discussion; occupancy-related expenses require further consideration. [CBRE, November 2025](https://www.cbre.com/insights/articles/hotel-food-and-beverage-a-bright-spot-in-2025)

### 3.3 What the different activities actually sell

The following is a business-analysis framework, not a measured ranking for every property.

| Activity | Revenue types | Main profitability question |
|---|---|---|
| Rooms | Individual leisure/business bookings, corporate contracts, group blocks, extended stays | What remains after acquisition costs, servicing and property overhead? |
| Restaurant and breakfast | Buffet, à la carte, breakfast packages, outside diners | Does demand cover kitchen and service staffing, ingredients and waste? |
| Bar/lounge | Drinks, snacks, packages | Is gross product margin sufficient after staffing, premises and operating hours? |
| In-room dining | Meals, delivery/service charges | Does the order value justify preparation, delivery and collection time? |
| Meetings/conferences | Venue, catering, equipment, associated room blocks | What is the total contribution across departments and displaced business? |
| Weddings/banquets | Packages, meals, drinks, venue, accommodation | What remains after setup, service, teardown, overtime and package inclusions? |
| Other amenities | Spa, parking, laundry, recreation, retail or concession rent | Is the hotel operating the service itself or earning rent/commission? |

No consistent, comparable dataset reviewed here establishes that bars, weddings or conferences universally produce the highest net margin.

### 3.4 Why events cannot simply be ranked separately from F&B

An event is a demand segment as well as an operational activity. Its catering can appear under F&B, its guest bedrooms under rooms, and its venue/equipment income under the hotel's chosen reporting categories.

**Illustration:** a conference generates S$20,000 in bedrooms, S$15,000 in catering and S$5,000 in venue fees. Its total revenue is S$40,000, not S$40,000 plus those departmental amounts.

Analytical implication: compare total event contribution with the alternative use of the same rooms, space and staff. A discounted group can be valuable on quiet dates but displace better-paying business on busy dates. Revenue alone cannot resolve that comparison.

CBRE's 2024 cost analysis links a greater buffet/banquet share to lower food-cost ratios, while still identifying substantial labour and supply pressures. This supports investigating banquet economics, not assuming all events are high-profit. [CBRE, May 2025](https://www.cbre.com/insights/articles/all-eyes-on-operating-costs-in-2025-lessons-learned-in-2024)

## 4. How This Changes by Hotel Type

These are directional business-model interpretations; categories overlap and do not establish a universal ROI ranking.

| Hotel type | Revenue emphasis | Economic trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Economy/limited-service | Predominantly accommodation; limited add-ons | Simpler operations, but lower prices and local demand still matter |
| Midscale/upscale business hotel | Rooms, breakfast, corporate bookings and meetings | Corporate volume versus negotiated rates and service obligations |
| Convention/event hotel | Group rooms plus substantial catering and venue business | Cross-department revenue, but event volatility and large facilities |
| Luxury city hotel | Premium rooms, suites, dining, personalised service | Higher spending can come with high staffing and property costs |
| Leisure resort | Accommodation, dining, recreation and possibly spa | Several spending opportunities, but seasonality and extensive amenities |
| Extended-stay hotel | Longer accommodation bookings | Fewer guest turnovers and potentially leaner service; rate discounts |
| All-inclusive resort | Bundled accommodation, food, drinks and activities | Package price must cover consumption; departments need revenue allocation |
| Integrated casino resort | Hotel plus gaming/entertainment and other businesses | Whole-resort returns are not comparable with ordinary hotel returns |

**Evidence on extended stay:** HVS reviewed 60–150 historical operating statements per category, covering 2019 and 2021–2024. Economy extended-stay properties averaged GOP margins in the mid-50% range versus mid-40% for midscale. HVS defines GOP here after management fees but before property taxes, insurance, replacement reserves and other fixed items. These are US operating margins, not investment returns. [HVS, April 2025](https://www.hvs.com/article/10145-extended-stay-profitability-face-off-economy-vs-midscale)

Takeaway: the most expensive hotel does not necessarily have the best margin or the best return on invested capital.

## 5. The Hotel Business Model: Who Makes the Money?

The hotel name on the building does not necessarily identify the property owner.

| Role/model | How money is earned | Main economic exposure |
|---|---|---|
| Owner-operator | Guest revenue less operating and ownership costs | Property investment and operating performance |
| Property owner with external manager | Residual hotel cash flow after costs and management fees | Capital expenditure, financing and hotel performance |
| Management company | Base management fee and sometimes incentive fee | Contract terms, performance and retention |
| Franchisor/brand | Royalties and system-related fees | Brand strength, distribution and franchise network |
| Lessee/operator | Operating surplus after rent and other costs | Operating demand plus rental commitments |

Hilton's 2025 filing illustrates the distinction: franchise royalties generally depend on gross room revenue, sometimes other revenue; base management fees generally depend on gross operating revenue; incentive fees generally depend on operating profit. Its ownership business instead earns hotel sales. Therefore, brand-company economics should not be presented as the ROI of owning a hotel. [Hilton, 2025 Form 10-K](https://stories-editor.hilton.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Hilton-2025-Annual-Report-SEC-Form-10-K.pdf)

### 5.1 Property-level operating logic

A useful analytical model is:

1. Attract bookings through direct channels, intermediaries, corporate accounts and groups.
2. Allocate finite room inventory across dates, room categories and customer segments.
3. Deliver accommodation and associated services.
4. Earn additional contribution where appropriate.
5. Cover shared operations and ownership obligations.

For a stable available-room inventory:

**Room revenue = available room-nights × occupancy × average daily rate (ADR).**

RevPAR is room revenue per available room-night; total RevPAR includes other hotel revenues. Neither measures profit. [Host, operating-statistic definitions](https://www.hosthotels.com/Press-Releases/2026/PressRelease_27966)

An unsold room-night cannot be carried into tomorrow. Conversely, filling every room is not necessarily optimal if the additional booking has poor net contribution or overloads service capacity. This is the economic tension behind rate setting and workload planning.

## 6. Are Upsells Common and Important?

### 6.1 Established practice, but no universal adoption statistic

Yes—paid upgrades are an established commercial practice, including in major-chain digital channels. In June 2026, Hilton announced that eligible members could see paid and complimentary upgrades during digital check-in across its global portfolio. This demonstrates mainstream deployment, not the percentage of all hotels using upselling or its average profit contribution. [Hilton announcement](https://stories.hilton.com/hilton-honors/hilton-honors-redefines-the-room-upgrade-experience-for-members-with-more-choice-and-flexibility)

The Novotel learning manager also discussed paid upgrades, breakfast, early check-in and late checkout. This is interview evidence from the supplied notes, not an audited revenue result.

### 6.2 Upselling versus cross-selling

| Offer | More precise description | Capacity/cost issue |
|---|---|---|
| Standard room to suite | Upsell | Premium inventory and additional servicing |
| Higher floor/view category | Upsell where sold as a higher category | Availability and willingness to pay |
| Breakfast added to room-only booking | Cross-sell/add-on | Food, staffing and restaurant capacity |
| Early check-in/late checkout | Time-based add-on | Cleaning schedule and the next booking |
| Lounge access | Add-on or bundled upgrade | Staffing, food and lounge occupancy |
| Spa, transfers, dining package | Cross-sell | Delivery cost, commissions and availability |

Hotels may informally group these under “upselling,” but measuring them separately makes the economics clearer.

### 6.3 Why an upgrade can be financially attractive

**Illustration only:** a guest pays S$50 extra for a room upgrade. Additional servicing costs S$8 and a selling incentive costs S$2. The immediate incremental contribution is S$40, before any other incremental fees or displaced premium-room sale.

The full S$50 is not profit. Nor is the S$40 necessarily genuinely incremental if the guest would otherwise have paid the normal premium rate.

Upselling can raise spending from an already-acquired guest without requiring another occupied room. That is a plausible economic benefit, not evidence that every upsell programme performs well.

### 6.4 When upselling is less valuable or counterproductive

- The room would otherwise sell at a higher price.
- A paid offer duplicates an included or complimentary loyalty benefit.
- Late checkout blocks cleaning and the next arrival.
- Breakfast/lounge sales exceed service capacity.
- Extra selling lengthens queues or interrupts more urgent guest assistance.
- Staff incentives favour revenue while ignoring fulfilment costs.
- A discounted upgrade substitutes for a purchase the guest would already have made.

These are hypotheses to test at the visited properties, not confirmed failures.

### 6.5 How to measure importance properly

Track eligible opportunities, offers, acceptances, realised revenue, incremental delivery costs, incentives, fees, reversals and displaced sales. Measure conversion among guests actually offered an eligible product—not among all arrivals.

A programme's economic value is:

**Incremental revenue − incremental costs − lost contribution from displaced sales.**

Compare matched periods or controlled groups where feasible. A before/after increase can reflect occupancy, guest mix, seasonality or room-category availability rather than better selling.

## 7. Weddings: Revenue, Costs and Commercial Importance

### 7.1 What the evidence establishes

Weddings can generate substantial contracted sales for a ballroom hotel, but the available sources do not establish a representative Singapore wedding profit margin or prove that weddings outperform rooms annually. Published packages reveal pricing and inclusions; they do not reveal labour costs, negotiated discounts, utilisation or investment returns.

Wedding catering overlaps with F&B reporting. Associated bedrooms belong to the rooms business, and venue income depends on accounting policy. Analyse the wedding across departments without adding its total to the same departmental sales twice.

### 7.3 Relevance to Novotel Singapore on Stevens

Novotel's official site advertises three banquet tiers—Nova, Constellation and Galactical—alongside solemnisation and Indian wedding offerings. This establishes product segmentation, not which tier sells best. [Novotel weddings](https://www.novotel-singapore-stevens.com/weddings/)

Its FAQ states that minimum requirements depend on date, season and venue; typical service windows are noon–4pm and 6pm–11pm. It offers multiple banquet formats, AV upgrades and scheduled vendor loading/setup. Peak-date bookings are recommended 10–12 months ahead. These facts identify planning, space allocation and coordination requirements beyond food service. [Novotel wedding FAQ](https://www.novotel-singapore-stevens.com/weddings/wedding-faqs/)

### 7.4 Different wedding formats create different operational demands

The following implications are analytical hypotheses to test, not measured comparative margins.

| Format | Commercial structure | Possible pressure points |
|---|---|---|
| Small solemnisation | Venue/time package with light catering | Coordination cost spread over fewer guests; weather contingencies |
| Seated banquet | Per-table or per-person menu with minimum attendance | Simultaneous service, dietary exceptions, table assignment and course timing |
| Buffet or cocktail reception | Per-person catering and defined service window | Replenishment, consumption uncertainty and waste |
| Multi-session cultural celebration | Several ceremonies, meals or spaces | More handovers, room resets and schedule dependencies |
| Wedding with overnight guest group | Banquet plus additional room bookings | Cross-department coordination and competition for bedrooms |

Do not infer that every cultural wedding is multi-day or requires the same service format.

### 7.5 Why weddings can be commercially attractive

**Evidence:** CBRE's analysis of 2,600 US hotels associates a greater buffet/banquet share with lower food-cost ratios. However, 2024 F&B labour and operating-supply expenses rose 4.5% and 9.4%. Lower ingredient cost ratios therefore do not guarantee higher net profit. [CBRE cost study](https://www.cbre.com/insights/articles/all-eyes-on-operating-costs-in-2025-lessons-learned-in-2024)

**Economic interpretation of the package evidence:**

- Minimum table commitments create a contracted sales floor, subject to the actual contract.
- Pre-agreed menus and attendance allow advance production planning.
- One booking can purchase catering, space and related services.
- Date and package tiers support different price points.
- Paid enhancements can increase contribution from an already-won booking.

Deposits and cancellation terms could also affect cash flow and risk, but these were not verified in the reviewed packages. A deposit should not be treated as evidence of earned profit.

### 7.6 Where apparent profit can disappear

| Cost or constraint | What to investigate |
|---|---|
| Ingredients and drinks | Guaranteed versus actual attendance; consumption of unlimited drinks |
| Labour | Planning, kitchen, service, setup, teardown, agency premiums and overtime |
| Package inclusions | Rooms, tastings, décor, equipment, parking and other promised benefits |
| Coordination | Revisions, dietary lists, suppliers and information reaching temporary staff |
| Recovery/rework | Remade meals, substitutions, refunds or complimentary items |
| Space/time | Setup and teardown blocking other bookings |
| Shared costs | Sales teams, maintenance, utilities and allocated hotel overhead |
| Capital | Ballroom fit-out, kitchen capacity and periodic refurbishment |

A long banquet can consume more labour and block the next event. These are plausible links to the learning manager's reported overrun problem; their size and frequency remain unknown.

### 7.7 What published studies actually show

The direct research base is small. The studies below support narrower conclusions about sales, revenue, service quality or space management. None reports a representative net wedding margin or investment ROI for Singapore hotels.

| Study | Data and method | Result relevant to this project | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yin, Fang and Cheng (2022), *Frontiers in Psychology* | Logistic regression using 763 banquet enquiries at one five-star Hilton in Quanzhou, China, from August 2018 to January 2019; 44 cases were weddings | Product type, price and promotion were associated with whether a banquet enquiry converted. Weddings recorded 18 successes and 26 failures. The adjusted odds ratio for weddings relative to pure catering was 2.032, but its 95% CI was 0.947–4.362 and p=0.069 | At the conventional 5% level, the wedding-specific coefficient was not statistically significant. It measures sales conversion at one hotel, not wedding profit, ROI or Singapore performance |
| Utama, Tuwi and Mareni (2026), *Journal of Tourism Economics and Policy* | Multiple regression using 36 monthly observations from one anonymised Indonesian resort for 2022–2024: counts of weddings and MICE events versus F&B revenue | Weddings and MICE together accounted for a reported 44.5% of variation in monthly F&B revenue; the authors report that weddings generated more revenue per occasion, whereas MICE was more frequent | The 44.5% is combined explanatory power, not “44.5% of revenue caused by weddings.” Event size, pricing, seasonality and other omitted factors may confound the relationship. It measures revenue, not cost, profit or ROI |
| Kimes and McGuire (2001), *Cornell Hotel and Restaurant Administration Quarterly* | Singapore function-space case study | Establishes function-space revenue management as a hotel problem and argues that function spaces can be managed to increase their revenue contribution | It is not a current wedding-margin benchmark; the repository record does not establish a general effect size |

#### Interpretation

The safest conclusion is that weddings are a commercially meaningful banquet product, but the research does not support a universal claim that they are the most profitable hotel activity.

The Quanzhou sales study is useful for understanding conversion. The Indonesian resort study is the only direct revenue analysis found, but its 36 observations and combined wedding/MICE model make it unsuitable for estimating wedding ROI. The service studies support the operational concern from the visits: outcomes depend on coordinated delivery, facilities, staff performance and guest care.

**Our analytical implication:** evaluate contribution per occupied ballroom-hour or square-metre-hour, including setup and teardown, alongside total event contribution. Also record service failures, overtime and recovery costs. A larger bill may involve more space, longer occupation and greater delivery cost.

### 7.8 How to compare weddings, conferences and rooms fairly

Use the same period, avoid duplicate revenue and state the included expenses:

**Event contribution = event revenue excluding GST − event-attributable delivery costs.**

For an accept/reject decision, also consider contribution displaced from alternative bookings. For annual profitability, deduct the relevant shared/fixed costs; for investment ROI, include capital expenditure and the timing of cash flows.

An included suite has servicing cost even when it would otherwise be empty. If it would otherwise sell, it can additionally carry an opportunity cost. Its published retail price is neither automatically its delivery cost nor guaranteed foregone revenue.

A S$60,000 wedding bill cannot be fairly compared with one day's bedroom revenue without considering event frequency, room inventory, delivery costs and shared expenses. A busy Saturday does not establish annual ballroom utilisation.

### 7.9 Wedding upselling: available offers versus proven returns

The official packages demonstrate real menu tiers and add-ons. They do not disclose conversion rates or incremental margins.

| Offer | What determines incremental value |
|---|---|
| Higher menu tier | Price difference less extra ingredients and preparation |
| Food station or late-night food | Additional sales less equipment, staffing and food |
| Premium drinks | Paid spending beyond included beverages |
| AV enhancement | Whether income belongs to the hotel or an external vendor; related costs |
| Additional accommodation | Room contribution and displaced higher-value bookings |
| Extra event time | Added fees versus overtime and blocked venue availability |

The relevant sales interaction is with the couple/planner during event planning. This differs from room upgrades offered to arriving guests. Vendor-provided services must not be counted as hotel revenue unless the hotel actually earns a fee or operates the service.

### 7.10 Questions that would establish the actual business case

1. How many weddings occur annually, and what is their share of banquet and total hotel contribution?
2. What percentage of ballroom sessions are sold, including quieter dates?
3. What is the average realised package price after negotiated concessions?
4. Which inclusions and changes most often exceed their cost allowance?
5. What are actual labour hours, agency costs and overtime per wedding?
6. How frequently do overruns, dietary-information errors or service failures occur?
7. What information do part-time staff lack at the start of service?
8. Which enhancements are purchased most often, and what contribution remains after fulfilment?
9. What profitable bookings are rejected because space, staff or kitchen capacity is unavailable?
10. Are sales teams assessed on contracted revenue, realised revenue or realised contribution?

The defensible problem hypothesis is: **wedding revenue may be weakened by gaps between what sales teams promise, what operations plan and what event staff can deliver under time pressure.** The visits suggest relevant staffing and overrun issues; follow-up evidence is needed before treating this as a confirmed shared problem.

## 8. Implications for the Two Visits

### Novotel/Mercure on Stevens

The learning manager reportedly ranked rooms first and F&B second in revenue. External research supports the plausibility of this order; it does not establish either property's actual percentages.

The shared 772-room complex should not be treated as 772 identical revenue opportunities. Room categories, brands, corporate agreements, included benefits and available inventory can change eligibility.

Reported room-release delays and housekeeping pressure create a relevant question: are lost selling opportunities caused by weak sales technique, unavailable inventory, or an inability to deliver extra services?

### PARKROYAL

The notes connect attentive service and upselling with emotional intelligence and sustained observation. The business question is whether fatigue causes measurable missed opportunities, service failures, or both.

Part-time event staffing may affect event contribution through overtime, rework or recovery costs. These mechanisms remain unverified.

For both visits, upselling is one possible financial consequence of operational problems—not proof that a sales problem should replace the established manpower and service-consistency problem.

## 9. Questions for Follow-up Interviews

1. What share of revenue and departmental profit comes from rooms, outlets and banqueting?
2. Where are venue hire, service charges and event-related room bookings recorded?
3. Which hotel expenses are excluded from the departmental figures?
4. What are the most common paid add-ons, and which produce the most incremental contribution?
5. How many guests are eligible, offered an upgrade and converted?
6. What proportion already receive breakfast, lounge access or upgrades through packages or loyalty?
7. Do housekeeping readiness or room availability prevent sales?
8. Does selling at check-in measurably increase handling time or queues?
9. What costs rise when events overrun?
10. Who owns, operates and brands the property, and who approves operating expenditure or capital investment?
11. Is staff time released by a change actually converted into lower overtime, avoided recruitment or additional profitable service?
12. What return measure and payback threshold does the decision-maker use?

## 10. Evidence Limits

- Most quantitative departmental evidence here is from US hotels, not Singapore.
- Portfolio averages cannot establish the profitability of Novotel or PARKROYAL.
- The financial benchmarks are industry disclosures and practitioner studies. The wedding section also includes historical academic research records; none establishes a causal effect of upselling on satisfaction or loyalty.
- No representative cross-industry upsell adoption rate was established.
- No universal investment ROI or reliable net-margin ranking for weddings, conferences, restaurants and bars was established. The direct wedding studies found are single-property or perception studies and do not report net margins.
- Hypothetical examples are explicitly labelled and must not be presented as visit findings.
- This document provides financial context; it does not endorse a particular technology or intervention.
