# Recurring Hotel Problems — Novotel and PARKROYAL Literature Review

Research date: 17 September 2026  
Project: CDE4301  
Scope: Problem discovery, supporting evidence and further investigation; no solution proposals.

## 1. Main Finding

The most defensible shared problem is:

**Hotel employees must deliver timely, informed and personalised service while their attention is divided between operational work, guest-information handovers and individual guest needs.**

This is a synthesis of the two visits, not a direct quotation or a proven causal explanation. The visits suggest several distinct difficulties within this broader problem: workload pressure, information not reaching the next employee, guest recognition requiring manual effort, privacy constraints and inconsistent service readiness.

“Both hotels lack technology” would be an inaccurate conclusion. Both already use digital systems. The question is whether the necessary information, staff time and authority are available at the moment of service.

## 2. Sources and Review Method

### Visit evidence

- [Novotel formatted notes](novotel-formatted.md): live-typed paraphrases of the Learning Manager, observations and subsequent clarifications.
- [Novotel raw notes and group summary](raw-notes/novotel-raw-notes.md).

Some existing formatted notes contain analyst interpretations as well as reported statements. This review distinguishes those interpretations rather than treating every sentence as independent interview evidence.

### Literature-search approach

This is a targeted narrative review, not a systematic review or an exhaustive inventory. Searches covered hotel staffing shortages, emotional labour, employee knowledge sharing, hotel self-service failures, personalisation/privacy and named hotel examples. Sources were selected for relevance, identifiable methods and accessible evidence; government and regulator material, original research and first-hand industry surveys were prioritised. A regional news report supplies named interview examples.

Eight external sources are retained below. Academic coverage includes a meta-analysis, two empirical studies and a systematic review with survey evidence. Publication dates and study contexts matter: older studies illuminate mechanisms, not current staffing rates. Some official digital-check-in and PDPC pages could not be retrieved reliably; their inaccessible contents are not used as evidence.

## 4. Literature Review by Problem

### 4.1 Workload pressure and insufficient operational capacity

**Visit evidence.** Novotel explicitly identified manpower as its main problem. PARKROYAL described work that occupies bell staff and a person stationed to direct departing guests. The common issue is competing demands on employees, even if the underlying recruitment conditions differ.

**External evidence.** AHLA and Hireology surveyed 282 US hoteliers between 6 December 2024 and 3 January 2025. Of respondents, 65% reported staffing shortages and 71% had vacancies they could not fill despite active recruitment. Housekeeping and front-desk shortages were reported by 38% and 26%, respectively. These are respondent figures, not Singapore estimates. The association survey establishes wider recurrence, but does not demonstrate a representative global prevalence or identify the causes at either visited hotel. [S1](https://www.ahla.com/news/65-surveyed-hotels-report-staffing-shortages)

Singapore’s 2023 hotel Jobs Transformation Map was developed with input from 105 hotels and eight educational institutions. It examined 87 roles and identified 40 for likely redesign, while also identifying changing workforce aspirations and guest expectations. This establishes local sector relevance, but is not a survey showing that a particular percentage of Singapore hotels is understaffed. [S2](https://www.stb.gov.sg/about-stb/media-publications/media-centre/launch-of-jobs-transformation-map-to-identify-skills-and-talent-to-grow-hotel-industry/)

**Interpretation.** Separate three possible explanations:

- Too few employees overall.
- Enough employees in total, but insufficient coverage at particular times or in particular roles.
- Employees are available, but time is absorbed by coordination, repeated requests or exceptions.

These explanations require different evidence. A queue alone cannot distinguish them.

**Further learning.** Compare arrivals, departures, requests and staff availability in short time intervals. Ask which tasks stop when an employee becomes a runner, and whether late room release reflects cleaning, inspection or information-update delays.

### 4.2 Information handovers are vulnerable under pressure

**Visit evidence.** Novotel’s challenge concerns coordination across departments and shifts. PARKROYAL’s concerns a name relayed between two employees during arrival. In both, collecting information does not guarantee that the next employee receives and uses it.

**Research.** Pahi and colleagues surveyed 235 frontline employees across 37 hotels. Their model linked knowledge sharing with the relationship between leadership and commitment to service quality. It supports investigating organisational information flow, but does not measure earpiece failures or WhatsApp omissions. Causal claims are limited by the survey design. Reporting also warrants caution: the paper accepts a direct leadership hypothesis despite reporting p = .119, and includes inconsistent wording about some paths. It is corroborative, not decisive evidence. [S3](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.926779/full)

**Interpretation.** A missed message could result from audibility, interruption, unclear responsibility, missing acknowledgement or information arriving too late. These should not be collapsed into “poor communication.”

A useful unit of investigation is one handover: who knew what, when they passed it on, who received it, whether it was acknowledged, and what happened next.

**Further learning.** Ask each hotel for a recent specific missed-handover incident and a successful one. Distinguish a guest name used for a greeting from an allergy or unresolved service request, because the consequences differ substantially.

### 4.3 Digital services leave manual coordination and exceptions

**Visit evidence.** Novotel has integrated systems but still needs people to review profiles and monitor requests. At PARKROYAL, a QR process applies only to guests without outstanding balances, so a staff member directs guests to the appropriate route.

**Research.** Lu QianTing, Hee Chung Chung and Namho Chung analysed 370 responses from people with experience using hotel AI-based kiosks, collected in May 2020. The study examined service failures, recovery, expectations and satisfaction; failures included technical issues, interface difficulties and excessive waiting. Findings link recovery and expectations to satisfaction. This is evidence that self-service does not remove the possibility of service failure—not a measurement of staff workload or PARKROYAL’s QR process. The pandemic-era sample also limits transferability. [S4](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-65785-7_10)

**Interpretation.** “Manual” is not automatically “wasteful.” Checking an exception or making a room decision may require legitimate judgement. The research problem is whether employees spend time on avoidable rechecking and routing, or on decisions necessary for service quality.

**Further learning.** Measure staff assistance per checkout, eligibility for self-checkout, reasons for counter referral and repeated steps. At Novotel, distinguish time spent locating information from time spent interpreting it. Neither visit demonstrates that automation has failed.

### 4.4 Recognition depends on usable knowledge at the point of service

**Visit evidence.** Novotel stores extensive preferences, yet F&B staff may lack time to consult ACDC and rely on memory or a VIP board. PARKROYAL discussed identifying lounge guests through room-card entry. The shared challenge is connecting a present guest with relevant information at the right moment.

**Interpretation informed by Sections 4.2 and 4.3.** Data availability, identity matching, comprehension and action are separate stages. A hotel can have a detailed profile and still fail to deliver personalised service because the serving employee does not know that the person has arrived.

This is a working explanation derived from the visits. The selected literature does not quantify this precise failure across Singapore hotels.

**Further learning.**

- Which interactions genuinely require proactive recognition?
- What proportion of returning guests are not recognised, and do they care?
- How often is a preference outdated or context-dependent?
- Is the bottleneck identifying the guest, finding the record or having time to act?
- Are customer benefits measured, or inferred from repeat stays and absence of complaints?

Do not infer demand for facial recognition or smart glasses from a need for recognition. Do not interpret detailed profiles as proof of improved loyalty: frequent business travel and membership tier may also explain repeat stays.

### 4.5 Personalisation and privacy create a continuing tension

**Visit evidence.** Both visits raised restrictions around guest information. Novotel described role-based access alongside group-wide profiles; PARKROYAL described identity collection and a seven-day purge. The starting point of those seven days and the categories deleted remain unclear.

**Research.** Hsu, Parry and Ng’s 2026 paper combines a systematic review with survey evidence about personal data stores. It treats personalisation and privacy as persistent competing demands, rather than a one-time choice. Its conceptual relevance is strong, but it spans information systems, marketing and management rather than hotel operations specifically. It does not establish the acceptability of hotel facial recognition, VIP boards or either visited hotel’s practices. [S5](https://doi.org/10.1111/exsy.70314)

**Documented hotel case.** In December 2024, the US FTC finalised an order concerning Marriott and Starwood following charges over security failures and three breaches affecting more than 344 million customers worldwide. Exposed categories included passport, payment and loyalty information. This demonstrates consequential hotel-data risk; cybersecurity failures are not the same as lawful internal personalisation or cross-property access. It provides no evidence of wrongdoing at Novotel or PARKROYAL. [S6](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/12/ftc-finalizes-order-marriott-starwood-requiring-them-implement-robust-data-security-program-address)

**Further learning.** Establish who may view each field, what guests understand about observation-based profiling, how corrections are handled, and what deletion covers. Do not describe the seven-day period as a universal PDPA requirement. This review makes no legal compliance determination.

### 4.6 Attentiveness, emotional labour and consistent service

**Visit evidence.** PARKROYAL explicitly emphasised reading guests’ emotional states and the difficulty of maintaining attention after long hours. Novotel reported guest conflict, workload and service standards, but did not provide a fatigue assessment.

**Research.** Xu, Cao and Huo’s 2020 hospitality-and-tourism meta-analysis combined 57 correlation matrices. It found emotional labour related to employee and customer-related outcomes, with strain mediating relationships. This is broader support than one property anecdote, but associations and meta-analytic modelling do not diagnose burnout in individual workers. The accessible university abstract, rather than the full manuscript, was used here. [S7](https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Antecedents-and-Outcomes-of-Emotional-Labour/99516756802346)

**Interpretation.** Emotional intelligence means recognising and responding to emotions; emotional labour concerns managing emotional expression for work. Fatigue is not identical to either, and burnout should not be inferred simply from a long shift.

The combined evidence suggests investigating whether attentive service becomes harder under workload, not assuming that staff lack empathy or training.

**Further learning.** Compare the beginning and end of shifts, break access, interruptions and difficult guest encounters. Ask employees what prevents them acting on a need they already noticed. For part-time event staff, compare experience, briefing quality and role clarity before attributing service differences to employment status.

## 6. Synthesis: What the Evidence Supports

The sources operate at different levels:

- Industry surveys establish that staffing difficulty occurs beyond these properties.
- Hotel employee research helps explain why information sharing and sustained emotional work matter.
- Self-service research shows why a digital service must still be assessed as a complete guest interaction.
- Privacy research and a regulator case illustrate different aspects of information responsibility.
- Named hotel reporting shows variation and cautions against claiming a universal crisis.

Together, they support studying how limited staff capacity interacts with information-dependent service. They do not prove that adding staff, collecting more data or introducing technology will resolve the observed issues.

### Candidate problem statements

1. **Missed service information:** During busy periods and shift changes, guest information may not reach the employee responsible for the next interaction, causing repeated questions or missed personalisation.
2. **Competing demands on staff time:** Employees must complete operational tasks while noticing guest needs and managing exceptions, making timely, attentive service difficult to sustain.
3. **Profiles that are difficult to use in live service:** Relevant preferences may be recorded but not readily connected to the guest currently being served.
4. **Uneven service readiness:** Employees with different experience, language ability and briefing exposure may interpret and execute service requirements inconsistently.
5. **Unclear boundaries for personalisation data:** Useful guest information must be available to appropriate staff without unnecessary exposure or use beyond guest expectations.

These are research formulations, not verified causal findings or solution requirements.

## 7. Highest-Value Next Research

| Priority | Question | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Where does a handover actually fail? | Trace several guest journeys across employees; record acknowledgement, delay and consequences without collecting guest identifiers. |
| 2 | Which tasks consume peak-period capacity? | Task timings, staffing coverage, request arrivals and interruptions. |
| 3 | Does the profile help the employee serving the guest? | Anonymised walkthroughs separating identification, retrieval, interpretation and action. |
| 4 | Is service inconsistency related to employment status or preparation? | Comparable tasks across experience levels, briefing exposure and shift conditions. |
| 5 | Does attentiveness change over a shift? | Voluntary staff accounts and repeated observations, avoiding unsupported clinical labels. |
| 6 | What are the actual guest-data boundaries? | Explanations of access roles, retention start points, permitted uses and guest notices—not live guest records. |

Start by validating handovers and workload because both visits provide concrete examples. Emotional fatigue and the effects of temporary staffing remain important, but require more direct employee evidence.

Before broader comparison, confirm the PARKROYAL property and interview role; resolve Novotel’s unverified housekeeping hours/quota arrangement; and obtain frequency and severity estimates rather than relying only on memorable anecdotes.
