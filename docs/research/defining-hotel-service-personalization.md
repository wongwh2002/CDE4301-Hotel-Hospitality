# Defining personalization in hotel service

**Research date:** 2026-10-09
**Purpose:** Establish a useful, source-grounded meaning of personalization for the hotel guest-context project and distinguish it from customization, recognition, and generally good service.

## Short assessmen

Researchers use related terms in different ways, so there is no single clean boundary across all service and marketing research. A hotel-specific scale treats personalization as a customer–employee construct with four dimensions: responsiveness, intimacy, empathy, and attention to personal interests. Other service research describes its social content, while marketing research distinguishes firm-led tailoring from customer-led customization. These definitions overlap: empathy and responsiveness can also be elements of good service, and greeting someone by name can be one personalized gesture.

For this project, the most useful operational definition is:

> **Guest-context personalization is when a frontline employee uses relevant knowledge about an individual guest’s needs, preferences, or circumstances to adapt the interaction or service being delivered, with the aim of making it more relevant to that guest. Whether the guest perceives it as a fit is a separate outcome to measure.**

This is a project definition synthesized from the sources, not a claim that the literature has one canonical definition. It separates the data or recognition step from the service action and the guest’s perception of fit.

## Distinctions that matter for the projec

### Personalization and customization

Arora et al. distinguish two forms of one-to-one marketing: **personalization** is when the firm decides what is suitable for an individual, usually using information about that person; **customization** is when the customer proactively specifies elements of the offering. In a hotel, adapting service based on a recorded food preference is personalization. Letting a guest choose among listed menu, pillow, or room options is customization. In practice, both can coexist—for example, a hotel may recommend an option based on a profile and let the guest choose. [Arora et al. (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11002-008-9056-z)

### Personalization and recognition

Recognition means knowing or identifying who the guest is, or recalling a name. Hotel-service literature includes addressing a guest by first name among possible personalized gestures, alongside remembering preferences and adapting the interaction. So recognition can contribute to personalization or signal it. But an identity match by itself does not show that staff used relevant context to change what they said or did. For this project, measure these separately:

1. **Recognition:** Was the returning guest identified or remembered?
2. **Context access:** What relevant information could staff retrieve, and was it usable in time?
3. **Adaptation:** Did the employee adjust the interaction or service based on that information?
4. **Perceived fit:** Did the guest notice and experience the action as relevant to them?

This distinction keeps a face or name match from being mistaken for evidence that the hotel delivered personalized service. Hotel research describes both human gestures and technology that helps employees access or act on guest preferences. [Hiezl & Gyurácz-Németh (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsc.2663)

### Personalization and generally good service

Reliable, timely, courteous service is a baseline; personalization adds individual relevance or social attention. The concepts are not fully separate. Mittal and Lassar define personalization through the social content of employee–customer interaction. In their survey of 233 adult consumers, it was especially influential in interactive service encounters, but its influence was subsumed in other service-quality dimensions for services performed on a physical possession. In a hotel scale, responsiveness and empathy appear as personalization dimensions, illustrating the overlap. [Mittal & Lassar (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-4359(96)90007-X) · [Choo (2012)](https://journal.kci.go.kr/JTS/archive/articleView?artiId=ART001697043)

## What the hotel research supports

Choo developed a hotel service-personalization scale for customer–employee relationships and identified **responsiveness, intimacy, empathy, and personal interests** as four factors. This is directly relevant to hotel service, but it should be treated as one study’s measurement model rather than a universal taxonomy. In particular, responsiveness and empathy also describe good service more broadly. [Choo (2012)](https://journal.kci.go.kr/JTS/archive/articleView?artiId=ART001697043)

Lei et al. studied the gap between **actual** and **perceived** personalization in hotels through three sequential studies: qualitative inquiries, an online experiment with **919 participants**, and a survey with **214 participants**. Their work identifies service effort and surprise as factors that can increase perceived personalization. It supports measuring what the guest experiences, not only what the hotel intended. It does not establish that surprise is required, or that personalization raises hotel profit. [Lei et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2023.104818)

## Conditions and limits

- **Information alone is not personalization.** A profile, database, or recognition result is an input. The employee or service needs to use relevant context in an action.
- **Intent is not the same as perceived fit.** A hotel can attempt a tailored gesture that a guest does not notice or find relevant. Track the action and the guest response separately.
- **More is not always better.** Service research describes a trade-off between efficiency and personalization; expectations and service context affect the appropriate degree. Different personalization strategies do not all improve satisfaction. [Surprenant & Solomon (1987)](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224298705100207)
- **Definitions overlap and vary by setting.** The distinctions here are practical working distinctions. Existing studies include both interpersonal warmth and data-based tailoring under personalization.
- **The sources do not validate the project’s technology choice.** They support examining how guest context becomes useful service; they do not show that face matching, smart glasses, or any particular access interface is needed.

## Project implication

The research question can focus on the steps between data and service:

> When a returning guest is being served, what relevant context do staff need, where is it available, can they retrieve it in time, and how does it change the service the guest receives?

Observe or ask about recent cases, recording recognition, context access, staff action, and guest-perceived relevance as distinct things. This can reveal whether the underlying obstacle is identification, fragmented or difficult-to-retrieve information, interpretation, timing, handover, or lack of opportunity to act.
