# Guest name recognition: experience and commercial evidence

**Research date:** 2026-10-10
**Purpose:** Examine why being recognized or addressed by name may feel good, when name use can backfire, and whether research links it to commercial outcomes relevant to a hotel personalization project.

## Short assessmen

Using a guest's name can make an interaction feel more personal because it signals that the employee is responding to a person rather than processing an interchangeable order. A recent retail field study found a small improvement in service satisfaction when café customers were addressed by name; a 2026 restaurant field experiment found stronger satisfaction and upselling results when server and guest exchanged names. The studies suggest rapport and reduced objectification as mechanisms.

The effect depends on delivery. Incorrect, over-frequent, privacy-sensitive, or overtly sales-driven name use can feel uncomfortable or manipulative. Name recognition is therefore a possible cue for a personal interaction, not personalization by itself: the staff member still needs to use relevant context to improve the service.

There is **no direct evidence identified here that hotel staff using returning guests' names increases hotel profit or GOP**. The financial results are from cafés, restaurants, or university dining facilities. They measure satisfaction, order acceptance, tips, spending, or revenue—not hotel profit after service and technology costs. Treat them as evidence that interpersonal recognition may affect customer response, not as a hotel ROI forecast.

## More direct hotel evidence

### Hotel guest preferences, service ratings, and repeat booking behavio

Piccoli, Lui, and Grün (2017) studied an independent **four-star, 122-room hotel** whose guests were about 60% business travelers. The hotel invited guests to record room and stay preferences through an IT-enabled page, then used them to prepare the stay. The researchers analyzed **98,330 reservations from 2010–2014**, including **4,706 linked Booking.com reviews**. Guests who used the preference system had **21.1% higher odds of a higher service-rating category** and **19.8% higher odds of a higher value-rating category**. For guests returning after an initial OTA booking, first-stay use was associated with **61.3% higher odds of switching to the hotel website for the second stay**. For guests returning after initially booking on the hotel's website, it was associated with **32.2% lower odds of switching to an OTA**.

This is the strongest hotel-specific performance evidence for the broader project premise: capturing guest context and enabling staff to use it may improve ratings and channel economics. However, it is a single-hotel observational study with self-selection; the results are changes in odds, not percentage-point gains or profit margins. It tested a preference page and hotel service fulfillment, not name recognition, face matching, or smart glasses.

Source: Piccoli, G., Lui, T.-W., & Grün, B. (2017). “The impact of IT-enabled customer service systems on service personalization, customer service perceptions, and hotel performance.” *Tourism Management*, 59, 349–362. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2016.08.015) · [Open author manuscript](https://dds.cct.lsu.edu/ddslab/pdf/piccoliLuiGruen.pdf).

Bonaretti et al. (2020) extend the hotel evidence with a longitudinal field study using **118,647 reservations from a network of seven independently run, three- and four-star hotels in Switzerland**. They examine how a customer service system’s digital representation of preferences supports frontline staff in eliciting and using preferences to personalize service. The authors report improved customer value, but the study does not test name or face recognition and its accessible abstract does not report a hotel profit estimate.

Source: Bonaretti, D., Bartosiak, M., Lui, T.-W., Piccoli, G., & Marchesani, D. (2020). “What can I(S) do for you? How technology enables service providers to elicit customers’ preferences and deliver personalized service.” *Information & Management*, 57(6), 103346. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.im.2020.103346) · [Publisher abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378720620302846).

### What frequent luxury-hotel guests say they value

Bowen and Shoemaker surveyed **892 luxury-hotel guests**, most of whom traveled frequently and primarily for business, about relationship-marketing benefits. Guests supported benefits such as personalized service, specific-room requests, expedited registration, flexible check-in/check-out, and employees taking their problems seriously. This is relevant to the high-end and repeat-traveler framing, but it records stated support for loyalty benefits rather than observed repeat stays or profit.

Source: Bowen, J. T., & Shoemaker, S. (1998). “Loyalty: A Strategic Commitment.” *Cornell Hotel and Restaurant Administration Quarterly*, 39(1). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/001088049803900104).

Buehring and O’Mahony’s study of **25 frequent luxury-hotel guests** found that being acknowledged and recognized personally stood out as a guest-valued experience generator. It supplies a guest-side rationale for recognition in luxury settings, but its small, purposively selected interview sample cannot establish how common that preference is or whether it changes spending or loyalty.

Source: Buehring, J. H., & O’Mahony, B. (2019). “Designing memorable guest experiences: Development of constructs and value generating factors in luxury hotels.” *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Insights*, 2(4), 358–376. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/JHTI-11-2018-0077) · [Open manuscript](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/92274/1/JHTI_Memorable_Experiences_Buehring_OMahony_Apr_2019.pdf).

### Hotel-specific evidence on how staff address guests

Fan et al. (2023) ran two randomized hotel-letter scenario experiments: one after successful service (**142 participants**) and one after a service failure (**147 participants**). Participants saw either a formal salutation (title and surname) or a casual one (first name). After successful service, the preferred style depended on participants’ power-distance orientation. After service failure, formal address produced higher satisfaction regardless of that orientation. This directly tests address style in a hotel context, but it is not a test of staff recognizing repeat guests or using profile data; it measures stated satisfaction, not actual hotel behavior or financial outcomes.

Source: Fan, A., Van Hoof, H. B., Dou, X., & Serrano, A. (2023). “The impact of linguistic style on customer satisfaction: a cross-cultural empirical study in Ecuador.” *International Hospitality Review*, 37(1), 125–142. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/ihr-04-2021-0028) · [Open article PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/4f22/8d58f951d93be9901e8356f4b681180a25f7.pdf).

## Why recognition can feel good

Lim et al. (2024) propose that name-based identification communicates that a customer is an individual rather than just an order or source of revenue. In their experiments, customers reported feeling less objectified when their orders were identified by name rather than number. In a two-week field study at two campus coffee shops, surveyed customers also gave slightly higher service-satisfaction ratings when staff called out names rather than order numbers: **5.35 vs. 5.13 on a seven-point scale** (1,120 responses; *p* = .008; small effect, ηp² = .01).

This offers a plausible explanation for why being recognized can feel affirming: it conveys attention and individuality, which can support rapport. It does not show that every guest wants name use, or that hearing a name is equivalent to staff remembering a meaningful preference.

Source: Lim, S., van Osselaer, S., Goodman, J. K., Fuchs, C., & Schreier, M. (2024). “The Starbucks effect: When name-based order identification increases customers’ store preference and service satisfaction.” *Journal of Retailing*, 100(2), 316–329. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2024.04.002) · [Author manuscript](https://bpb-us-w2.wpmucdn.com/u.osu.edu/dist/0/37067/files/2024/06/Lim-et-al-2024-JR-The-Starbucks-Effect.pdf).

## Does using a guest's name improve outcomes?

Raja, Cheng, and Danaher (2026) tested name use during service in a suburban Australian restaurant over four weeks. Their field experiment covered **127 tables** across four conditions: no name exchange, the server using the customer's name, the server introducing themself by name, or a mutual name exchange. Compared with no name exchange, reported service satisfaction was higher in all name conditions (means **5.74** with no name, **6.65** when the server used the customer's name, **6.52** when the server gave their own name, and **6.91** when names were exchanged, on a seven-point scale).

The study also observed behavioral outcomes. The special-item upsell acceptance rate was **22.6%** with no name exchange, **48.4%** when the server used the customer's name, **35.5%** when the server gave their own name, and **64.7%** with mutual name exchange. The increase was statistically significant for customer-name use and mutual exchange; server-name introduction alone did not significantly increase acceptance.

Average sales per customer were **$28.67** in the no-name condition and **$35.73** with mutual name exchange, a roughly **25%** difference. However, the overall four-condition spending test was not statistically significant (*p* = .103); the planned mutual-exchange comparison was significant (*p* = .016). The customer-name-only comparison was marginal (*p* = .095). This is suggestive evidence from one small restaurant field experiment, not a robust general estimate.

In online experiments in the same paper, moderate name use supported satisfaction through rapport. Repeating a name too often increased discomfort, and using names alongside a sales pitch removed the advantage. These findings point toward natural, occasional use in genuine conversation rather than a scripted name in every sentence or a name used mainly to sell.

Source: Raja, S. V. S., Cheng, Y., & Danaher, T. S. (2026). “Call me by my name: The effects of addressing customers by their names, the underlying mechanisms, and the boundary conditions.” *Journal of Retailing*. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2026.03.008) · [Monash record and abstract](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/call-me-by-my-name-the-effects-of-addressing-customers-by-their-n/).

## What do the profit studies actually show?

The 2024 *Journal of Retailing* paper includes a separate, exploratory difference-in-differences comparison at two university dining facilities. After name-based order identification was introduced at one facility, its sales increased **5%** relative to Fall 2019, while sales at the comparison facility decreased about **2%**. The authors describe this as merely suggestive evidence. It is a short, single-institution comparison, and sales are revenue rather than profit; it does not account for implementation or labor costs.

The 2026 restaurant experiment measured upsell acceptance and bill sales per customer, not contribution margin or restaurant profit. An older field experiment by Seiter, Givens, and Weger covered **194 diners in 90 parties at two U.S. restaurants**. It combined servers introducing themselves and using diners’ names with name use, and the average tip rose from **17.32% to 23.55% of the bill** (a 6.23 percentage-point increase). Since the two behaviors were bundled, the separate effect of name use cannot be isolated. Tips are server income, not hotel or restaurant profit.

These studies are relevant because they measure customer behaviors close to commercial value—purchase acceptance, spending, or revenue. They do not establish that name use causes more profitable hotel stays. The distinction matters: additional sales can come with food, labor, discount, or technology costs, and an experience improvement can have value even without immediate incremental spend.

Sources: the Lim et al. paper above; Seiter, J. S., Givens, S. M., & Weger, H. (2016). “The Effect of Mutual Introductions and Addressing Customers by Name on Tipping Behavior in Restaurants.” *Journal of Hospitality Marketing & Management*, 25(5), 640–651. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/19368623.2015.1040140).

## Limits and implications for the hotel projec

- **Name recognition is not the same as useful personalization.** A name can create a small interpersonal cue. Remembering an allergy, resolving an open issue, or adapting a service to an expressed preference changes what the hotel does. Measure these separately.
- **Transfer to hotels is uncertain.** The direct name-use experiments are in cafés and restaurants; they do not study returning hotel guests, hotel lounges, or face matching. Luxury-hotel interviews do report that some frequent guests value being acknowledged and remembered, but that is qualitative evidence from a small, selected group—not a profit test. See [what hotel guests value in personalization](what-hotel-guests-value-in-personalization.md).
- **Accuracy and discretion matter.** The 2024 paper found that a wrong name could reduce satisfaction relative to using an order number. Its experiments also found that customers preferred number-based identification in a privacy-sensitive purchase setting. In hotels, staff should use a name only when confident and when it fits the guest's preferred level of formality and the setting.
- **Avoid name use as a sales trick.** The 2026 study found that name use tied to upselling can remove the rapport benefit. Names should support service, not disguise a pitch.
- **Test the service outcome, not only recognition.** In a lounge POC, record whether staff recognized the returning guest, whether they found relevant and current context, what they changed in the interaction, whether the guest perceived a benefit, and any operational or commercial outcome. Compare benefits with the time and cost required to deliver them.

For the broader hotel framing, a defensible statement is: **personal recognition may help guests feel individually attended to, but hotels need to turn accurate guest context into relevant, welcome service; the value and financial return of doing so should be measured in the actual service setting.**
