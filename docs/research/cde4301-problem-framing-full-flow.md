# CDE4301 report framing: from guest context to attentive hotel service

**Research date:** 2026-10-23
**Purpose:** Provide a report-ready problem narrative and a research-backed flow for the CDE4301 report. The project frames the problem at hotel level and uses the PARKROYAL lounge as a bounded proof-of-concept setting.
**Evidence rule:** Distinguish what published studies establish from what this team still needs to observe at PARKROYAL. In particular, do not present guests repeating preferences or staff-facing technology interrupting service as established, widespread facts.

## Recommended argument in one line

**Guests can value service that recognizes their individual needs; hotels may capture useful context, but information does not automatically reach the right employee at the right moment; staff-facing technology might help that handoff, but it can also change how attentive the interaction feels. The project should test this gap and trade-off in the PARKROYAL lounge before presenting them as local facts.**

## Report-ready problem-framing flow

### 1. Why personalize service?

Personalization matters when it makes a service more relevant to the guest, rather than simply adding a visible gesture. In interviews with 25 frequent luxury-hotel guests, Buehring and O’Mahony found that being acknowledged and recognized as an individual was a salient part of a memorable hotel experience. Their small, purposive sample supports the relevance of recognition in luxury hospitality, but it does not show that all hotel guests want the same level of attention or that recognition alone produces loyalty. A separate hotel study of perceived personalization found that guests’ ratings of different gestures—including name greetings and anticipating needs from past records—were fairly close, which cautions against treating first-name use as the definition of personalization. ([Buehring & O’Mahony, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1108/JHTI-11-2018-0077); [Lei et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2023.104818); see also [What hotel guests value in personalization](what-hotel-guests-value-in-personalization.md).)

For this report, use **professional rapport and individual recognition** rather than saying that a hotel should feel like a friendship. A guest’s name can be one signal of recognition, but its effect depends on how it is used: a 2026 study of name use in customer service found positive responses in some conditions and worse responses when names were overused, misused, or used in embarrassing contexts. More useful evidence of personalization is whether staff use relevant context to make an interaction or service fit the guest. ([Gremler & Gwinner, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1177/109467050031006); [Srinivasa Raja et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2026.03.008); [Defining hotel service personalization](defining-hotel-service-personalization.md).)

### 2. Hotels already have reasons and means to collect guest context

Hotel personalization can draw on information such as stated preferences, prior requests, current needs, and earlier conversations. A 2025 study developed a hotel measure that includes storing and updating guest data, offering products or information based on preferences, and recommending activities based on a previous conversation. Its survey of 105 hotels found variation in reported personalization-supporting technology. These are provider-reported practices and scale indicators; they do not show that every hotel stores each field or that frontline employees can retrieve it during service. ([Hiezl & Gyurácz-Németh, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsc.2663).)

There is promising hotel-specific evidence that preference capture can be associated with guest outcomes. At one four-star hotel, a guest preference system was associated with higher service and value ratings and differences in subsequent booking-channel behavior. Because guests selected whether to use the system and the analysis covered one hotel, it is evidence for a plausible business case—not proof that all profile systems improve hotel performance, or that a lounge display will do so. ([Piccoli, Lui, & Grün, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2016.08.015); [open author manuscript](https://dds.cct.lsu.edu/ddslab/pdf/piccoliLuiGruen.pdf).)

### 3. The issue to investigate is the last mile from data to service

Having information in a hotel system is different from an employee seeing, understanding, and acting on the relevant part of it during a guest interaction. A survey of 84 Greek hotels, followed by five interviews, described weak integration between hotel systems and customer databases, manual retrieval, and difficulties sharing informal guest knowledge across employees and time. The study is older and deliberately sampled hotels that said they were interested in personalization, so it shows a possible mechanism rather than a current prevalence estimate. ([Sigala, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2004.08.008); [author manuscript](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221409705_Customer_Relationship_Management_in_Hotels_Implementing_Customer_Relationship_Management_in_the_Hotel_Sector_Does_IT_always_matter).)

Other hotel evidence shows that a digital system alone does not guarantee service continuity. In a process-mapping case at one four-star hotel in Tirana, the hotel software connected departments and exposed customer information, yet the mapped request process lacked consistent shift-end reporting and completion feedback from housekeeping or maintenance to reception. This case concerns service requests rather than personalization profiles, and it cannot establish the PARKROYAL workflow. Together with interview research on how hotel employees share and apply guest knowledge, it supports examining the whole chain—where context is recorded, who can access it, how it is handed over, and what staff can do with it—not just the interface. ([Özdemir, Çolak, & Shmilli, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-018-0727-4); [institutional full text](https://gcris.agu.edu.tr/bitstreams/980ae729-6f3a-4222-8f24-858e3b0f9320/download); [Wavei & Lo, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/JHTI-01-2026-0070); [Hotel personalization in the moment of service](hotel-personalization-in-the-moment-service.md).)

### 4. What can we say about guests repeating themselves?

There is some direct evidence that repetition and repeated contact are relevant forms of guest effort, but the evidence does **not** establish that hotel guests commonly have to repeat preferences because staff cannot access a profile. Lu et al. developed a hotel consumer complaint-and-recovery effort scale using two samples of hotel guests who had complained about a service problem. Its time-related effort dimension includes repeat contacts with the company or service employees. This supports measuring repeated contacts as a possible cost of service recovery; it does not show that missing guest context caused the contacts. ([Lu et al., 2015/2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1096348015597033).)

An observational study of four UK four-star hotels also recorded front-desk situations where staff asked guests to repeat or spell their names, and cases where reservations could not be found because details were absent or had not transferred into the hotel system. This is direct evidence of some repeated identity or reservation details during check-in, but it is not evidence of guests repeating a food preference, allergy, or prior request in a lounge. A small 2025 thesis based on six interviews with hotel professionals in two hotels describes structured internal communication and PMS documentation as part of service recovery; it is a qualitative example of continuity practices, not evidence about how often guests must repeat complaints. ([Eggins, 2013 DBA thesis](https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/id/eprint/4590/1/Eggins13DBA.pdf), especially pp. 168–170 and 196–198; [Barimov, 2025 thesis record](https://www.theseus.fi/handle/10024/907833).)

Therefore, use repeated explanations as a **local research question and potential outcome**, not as a factual opening claim. In observation and interviews, distinguish a necessary confirmation—such as reconfirming a critical allergy—from avoidable repetition caused by a lost, unavailable, or unclear request. Record what was repeated, why, how often it occurred in the observed sessions, and whether the guest or staff considered it burdensome.

### 5. Does staff-facing technology interrupt service?

Technology can affect rapport, but the evidence is conditional. Giebelhausen et al. analyzed hotel guest-satisfaction data and ran an experiment. They found that technology used during a customer–employee exchange could reduce evaluations when an employee was actively building rapport, while it could improve evaluations when it gave customers a way to disengage from an unpleasant interaction. A related hotel lobby touchscreen field experiment found that guests preferred using a self-service screen when an employee was nearby but not directly engaging them. These studies concern technology in or near the **guest-facing service encounter**, including kiosks and touchscreens; they do not test a staff member looking up a guest profile or wearing smart glasses. ([Giebelhausen et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.13.0056); [Cornell Hospitality Report, *Cyborg Service*](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/50c34d43-22dd-48f7-9c78-3c16e75c38b3).)

The closest direct evidence for staff-facing device use comes from a 2026 paper on employees recording customer requests. Across five experiments, participants generally rated digital request-taking less favorably than handwritten note-taking, with perceived attentiveness identified as a mechanism. In one hotel-concierge scenario, participants watched a video of an employee recording a request on a tablet or by hand; in another experiment, verbally confirming the request after recording it mitigated the negative effect of digital note-taking. These findings are directly relevant to how a visible device may be interpreted, but the studies use controlled scenarios and videos rather than observing hotel employees retrieving stored profiles during live service. They support testing attentiveness and request confirmation, not a claim that screens always interrupt. ([Liu, Sheng, & Choi, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2025.105270).)

There are also counterexamples. A survey at one U.S. luxury hotel brand found employees perceived work mobile devices as relevant and helpful to job performance; it measured employees’ self-reported perceptions, not objective productivity or guest-facing attentiveness. In laboratory studies, heads-up display text disrupted concurrent visual-search tasks, but those tasks were not hotel service interactions. Taken together, the research says that an interface can support work and still affect perceived attention or task focus depending on when, how, and for what task it is used. ([Jeong, Lee, & Nagesvaran, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2016.05.003); [Lewis & Neider, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-016-0015-6).)

### 6. Bound the project to a hotel-wide question and a lounge test

The problem framing should remain hotel-level: how might frontline staff bring relevant, current, guest-approved context into an in-person service moment? The PARKROYAL lounge is the project’s agreed proof-of-concept setting because the team has permission to test there; it is not evidence that PARKROYAL currently has the information or workflow gap. The team should first map the lounge’s actual service process, existing information sources, staff roles, and guest permissions.

The current prototype concept can be presented as one candidate test of that broader question: a guest taps a lounge access card, with the tap intended to establish the in-lounge guest set, and an employee receives a concise cue linking a guest to relevant service context. For any biometric trial, use synthetic records or participants who have explicitly opted in; a lounge-card tap establishes access or presence, not consent to face identification. Facial matching and smart glasses are implementation hypotheses in the current proposal, not established requirements or an adopted architecture. The prototype should test whether identification plus a carefully selected cue helps staff act on relevant information while preserving the interaction; it should not treat recognition alone as successful personalization. ([Hotel Smart-Glasses POC working context](../agents/hotel-smart-glasses-poc.md); [current architecture proposal](../architecture/hotel-lounge-smart-glasses-poc.md); [Singapore PDPC biometric guide](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/other-guides/guide-to-biometric_17may2022.pdf); [Defining hotel service personalization](defining-hotel-service-personalization.md).)

#### Illustrative lounge prototype flow

1. **Tap and temporary presence:** The access event adds a test guest to the temporary lounge set. Keep this separate from biometric opt-in.
2. **Identify conservatively:** In an opted-in or simulated test, the glasses-camera pipeline attempts to match a visible guest to that temporary set. A stable, unique match may automatically trigger a cue, as proposed in the current architecture. If the match is uncertain, show no personal details and use the normal staff interaction.
3. **Show only useful context:** During a lounge food-or-drink interaction, display one or two short, current details, such as a usual coffee preference and a dietary note. An allergy icon or emoji can flag “check this,” but the display should identify it as a prompt to verify—not as an instruction or guarantee.
4. **Keep the guest in control:** Staff acknowledge the guest naturally and confirm the relevant detail when appropriate (especially an allergy or stale/ambiguous preference), then adapt or disregard the cue based on the conversation. Confirmation here is about the guest’s current need, not an extra identity-confirmation step in the normal device flow.
5. **End the session:** Remove the temporary guest association and clear cues at exit or expiry. For initial evaluation, use synthetic data or consented participants and avoid retaining face images.

This gives the report a testable interaction: **tap → identify → retrieve minimal context → staff interprets and confirms where needed → adapt the service → clear temporary state**. Evaluate both the benefit (relevance, speed, staff comprehension) and possible costs (wrong cues, unwanted familiarity, eye-contact changes, perceived attentiveness). The allergy example must remain a cue for the established verification process, never the sole basis for a food-safety decision. ([Current architecture proposal](../architecture/hotel-lounge-smart-glasses-poc.md); [Liu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2025.105270); [Singapore PDPC biometric guide](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/other-guides/guide-to-biometric_17may2022.pdf).)

## Proposed problem statement and HMW

### Report-ready narrative

Hotels may collect guest preferences, service history, and other context to make service more relevant to individual guests. Guest research suggests that being recognized as an individual can matter, while hotel studies show that guest information may be fragmented or difficult to share across systems, employees, and shifts. However, having data on file does not guarantee that the employee serving a guest can retrieve and use the relevant detail at the right moment. Evidence that guests repeat preferences specifically because staff lack profile access is limited, and the effects of staff-facing devices on attentiveness depend on how the device is used. This project will investigate the gap between guest context and its use in face-to-face hotel service, using the PARKROYAL lounge as a bounded setting to design and test an initial prototype. ([Buehring & O’Mahony, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1108/JHTI-11-2018-0077); [Sigala, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2004.08.008); [Özdemir et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-018-0727-4); [Lu et al., 2015/2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1096348015597033); [Liu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2025.105270).)

### How-might-we question

> **How might we help hotel frontline staff access and use relevant, guest-approved information at the right moment, so they can provide attentive, fitting service while keeping the face-to-face interaction natural?**

This is stronger than asserting that guests routinely repeat themselves or that screens necessarily interrupt service. It names the design opportunity, leaves the cause open for local discovery, and does not prescribe face recognition or smart glasses as the only solution.

## Questions the lounge research should answer

1. **Need and context:** What information do lounge staff currently have, where does it come from, and which service moments would make it useful?
2. **Availability and continuity:** Can staff access that information in time? What happens when it is missing, stale, unclear, or held by another employee or shift?
3. **Guest effort:** Do guests repeat a request or preference, ask for an update, or explain a need again? Was repetition required for safety/confirmation, or did the service process lose context?
4. **Action and perceived fit:** When staff do receive a cue, does it change what they do? Does the guest perceive the change as relevant and welcome?
5. **Interaction quality:** Does retrieving or displaying information change eye contact, response time, staff comprehension, or guest-perceived attentiveness? Does a brief verbal confirmation help?
6. **Reliability and permissions:** Is the displayed detail current and permitted for the staff role? How are uncertainty and incorrect identity matches handled without revealing a guest’s private information?

## Suggested early prototype evaluation

Use scripted or otherwise approved test cases to compare the existing process with a low-fidelity context cue. Measure the whole chain separately: correct recognition, successful access to relevant information, staff understanding, changed service action, guest-perceived fit, retrieval time, and interaction quality. Record both helpful cues and unnecessary or wrong cues. For any digital display condition, evaluate staff and guest perceptions of attentiveness and whether staff confirmation preserves the conversation. These measures follow from the evidence that identification, context access, service adaptation, and perceived personalization are distinct outcomes ([Defining hotel service personalization](defining-hotel-service-personalization.md)) and that digital request-taking effects may be mitigated by confirming the request ([Liu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2025.105270)).

## Evidence boundary at a glance

| Claim | Evidence status | Safe report treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Guests can value individual recognition and relevant service | Direct hotel guest evidence, including a small luxury-hotel interview study and hotel personalization studies | Give the rationale, while noting the population and method limits. |
| Hotels collect preferences and prior guest context | Hotel studies document capability indicators and examples; practices vary | Say hotels *may* collect such information; do not assume every field exists at PARKROYAL. |
| Information may fail to reach staff or survive handover | Direct but context-specific hotel evidence from survey/interviews and process case studies | Frame as a documented risk and a local workflow to map. |
| Guests often repeat preferences because staff cannot access profiles | Not established by the sources reviewed | Treat as a hypothesis; observe and interview without leading questions. |
| Staff-facing screens interrupt rapport | Evidence is mixed and task-dependent; new request-taking experiments suggest a perceived-attentiveness risk | Treat interaction quality as a design criterion to test, not a settled fact. |
| Smart glasses or facial recognition solve the gap | Not established by the personalization or service-technology studies | Present as a candidate prototype method, subject to comparison with alternatives and local validation. |
