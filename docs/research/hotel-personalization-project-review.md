# Hotel personalization project review

**Research date:** 2026-10-12; supplemental review **2026-10-15**
**Purpose:** Audit the strongest existing sources before choosing the hotel's problem framing. This is a source audit, not a finding about the project properties.

## Recommended direction

Keep the hotel-wide aim: help frontline staff use relevant guest context to deliver consistent, personally relevant service. Use a staffed club lounge as the first setting, with returning guests who already have usable preferences or service requests on record. Frequent business travelers are a useful subgroup to interview. Target upscale and luxury properties as a proposed market, while recognizing that the evidence below spans other hotel categories as well.

### Suggested problem statemen

> At hotels that record guest preferences and service history, frontline staff may not have relevant, current information at the moment they serve a guest. During busy periods and shift changes, reliance on memory, manual profile checks, and handovers may lead to repeated questions or missed preferences. We will investigate how reliably staff can access and act on guest context during service, starting with a club lounge.

This is a problem hypothesis. The hotel-wide scope describes the ambition; a lounge pilot establishes evidence for that workflow. Each additional department needs its own investigation.

Use the project's operational definition of personalization: **staff use relevant knowledge about an individual guest's needs, preferences, or circumstances to adapt the service**. Record recognition, context retrieval, service adaptation, and the guest's response separately. See [Defining hotel service personalization](defining-hotel-service-personalization.md).

## What the visits contribute

The [Novotel notes](../../hotel-visit/novotel-formatted.md#personalisation-in-food-and-beverage) supply the clearest local lead: the Learning Manager reported recognition from memory, insufficient time for ACDC checks during service, and inconsistent shift handovers. The physical VIP board was reported by the manager; the group did not view it. This supports investigating a gap between recorded context and its use during service. It does not establish the frequency of missed context or its effects on guests.

The [PARKROYAL notes](../../hotel-visit/parkroyal-main-points.md#vip-recognition-and-personalisation) establish interest in a club-lounge use case. Interest in glasses is evidence of stakeholder interest, not proof of workflow benefit. Confirm the exact property, interview role, existing access rules, and which information lounge staff actually need.

The Novotel notes describe that brand as midscale. Consequently, the visits do not establish a luxury-industry problem rate. An upscale/luxury focus should be explained as the initial market choice and validated at a matching property.

## Primary-source audi

### Business travelers' repeat hotel choice: BCD Travel (2025)

**Evidence:** Commercial, self-reported traveler survey.

BCD surveyed **1,035 business travelers** online in North America, Europe, and Asia Pacific in May 2025. **73%** selected the statement that they repeatedly use the same hotel when revisiting a destination. For their **top three** accommodation-choice factors, respondents selected location **77%**, employer policy **56%**, price **53%**, and prior experience **41%**. The sample typically used three-star **62%**, four-star **53%**, and five-star **12%** hotels; accommodation types allowed multiple selections. [Report, PDF pages 2, 5, 11, and 13](https://www.bcdtravel.com/wp-content/uploads/BCD-report-Traveler-insights-Hotel-booking-trends.pdf).

**Use:** Repeat business travelers are a plausible segment to investigate. **Qualify:** This is neither an observed retention rate nor a luxury-hotel estimate. **Avoid:** Attributing repeat choice to personalization; the survey does not identify that cause or establish representative sampling.

The same report also shows why repeat travel does not automatically mean hotel choice is a guest's own: 84% said they book through a company online booking tool, 73% said their employer sets hotel rate limits, and employer policy was a top-three choice factor for 56%. Location (77%) and price (53%) ranked above prior experience (41%). This makes business travelers a useful segment for studying service continuity, but interviews should ask who selects the hotel and what alternatives were actually available. [BCD report, pp. 7, 10–11](https://www.bcdtravel.com/wp-content/uploads/BCD-report-Traveler-insights-Hotel-booking-trends.pdf).

The report also says business travelers commonly use Wi-Fi (**88%**), breakfast (**80%**), and early check-in/late check-out (**47%**); **23%** reported using a lounge. These are usage figures, not evidence that guests want those services personalized. They suggest useful questions to test—especially predictable work and arrival needs—while warning against assuming a lounge is the most important hotel-wide personalization moment. [BCD report, p. 14](https://www.bcdtravel.com/wp-content/uploads/BCD-report-Traveler-insights-Hotel-booking-trends.pdf).

### Hotel preference capture and performance: Piccoli, Lui, and Grün (2017)

**Evidence:** Peer-reviewed observational study at one four-star hotel. Preference-system use was associated with higher service/value ratings. Among returning guests, channel analyses used separate groups: **4,013 initial OTA bookers** (**61.3% higher adjusted odds** of second-stay website booking), and **1,587 initial website bookers** (**32.2% lower odds** of second-stay OTA booking). Voluntary uptake and one-property data limit causal inference. These odds changes concern channel choice conditional on a guest already returning; they do not estimate whether personalization caused the return. [Author manuscript, §4.2, printed pp. 356–358, Tables 4–7](https://dds.cct.lsu.edu/ddslab/pdf/piccoliLuiGruen.pdf#page=8).

The paper's economic mechanism is plausible: a direct booking may avoid an OTA commission. It also gives an **illustrative 18% flow-through increase** using that property's **$125 average daily rate** and an assumed **25% OTA commission**. Treat this as a scenario calculation, not measured causal net profit or a luxury-hotel margin estimate; it does not establish the total cost of personalization or the hotel's direct-channel acquisition costs. [Discussion, printed pp. 358–359](https://dds.cct.lsu.edu/ddslab/pdf/piccoliLuiGruen.pdf#page=10).

### Hotel service and observed re-patronage: Hu et al. (2019)

**Evidence:** Peer-reviewed longitudinal analysis of online reviews, rather than a personalization intervention.

The publisher describes **521,083 TripAdvisor reviews** across **7,031 hotels**, tracing the same reviewers at the same property across visits. Its abstract reports that service quality matters more for re-patronage with repeated visits and that location matters for returning. Its discussion excerpt explicitly reports business travelers as more likely than leisure travelers to revisit the property. [Publisher abstract and section excerpts](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S026151771930072X) · [Author university record](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/evolving-customer-expectations-of-hospitality-services-difference/).

**Use:** General service and location as repeat-choice factors. **Qualify:** Repeat reviews capture a selected group and can miss unreviewed return stays. Full statistical tables were unavailable in this audit, so use no business/leisure effect size. **Avoid:** Treating service quality as synonymous with personalization or interpreting the review sample as a luxury-hotel retention estimate.

### Hotels' data-access barriers: Hapi and Revinate (2025)

**Evidence:** Vendor-sponsored practitioner survey; verified through the sponsors' release.

The release describes **nearly 200 hospitality professionals** across brands, management companies, and properties worldwide, surveyed April 14–May 7, 2025. It reports **49%** struggling to access data for revenue and operational decisions and **40%** identifying disconnected systems as their largest obstacle. [Sponsors' release, “Key Report Findings” and “Survey methodology”](https://www.revinate.com/press-releases/the-future-of-hotel-data-report-reveals-nearly-50-of-hoteliers-struggle-to-access-critical-data/).

**Use:** Supporting context for investigating data access and integration. **Qualify:** The inspected public pages did not expose the original questionnaire, exact denominators, response rate, or role/property mix; exact question wording remains unverified. **Avoid:** Recasting 49% as frontline staff unable to retrieve guest preferences or as the prevalence of this problem in luxury lounges. The vendors sell data-connectivity products, so their profit/loyalty claims are not independent outcome measurements.

## Claims to retain, narrow, or drop

| Proposed claim | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Useful guest preferences can support better service | Retain as a supported possibility; verify actual benefits in the selected workflow. |
| Repeated business visits create opportunities for service continuity | Retain as a segment rationale; establish the target property's guest mix and travelers' booking autonomy. |
| Hotel data may be difficult to access or connect | Retain as a problem hypothesis; the external survey does not establish the frontline bottleneck locally. |
| Personalization makes guests return | Narrow to a hypothesis; the cited personalization field study estimates ratings and channel choice among returners. |
| Personalization increases hotel profit | Narrow: one hotel study reports channel shifts among guests who already returned and estimates a commission-based flow-through scenario. Net profit after personalization and direct-channel costs remains unestablished. |
| High-end hotels have especially thin margins relative to budget hotels | Drop from this evidence chain: none of these sources establishes that comparison. |
| Smart glasses or face recognition are necessary | Keep as solution hypotheses; these sources do not compare them with simpler ways to surface guest context. |

The evidence supports testing whether current guest context reaches staff in time to change a useful service action. It does not establish a complete causal chain from recognition to personalization, repeat stays, and profit.

## Additional experience evidence

Medallia's survey of **1,749 U.S. hotel guests** includes recalled examples such as having stay details ready, issue follow-up, and guest choice in problem resolution. These are reported experiences, not a ranking of importance. They make useful candidates for investigating continuity across employees. The report is vendor-produced. [Original report](https://www.medallia.com/wp-content/uploads/pdf/resources/Medallia-Understanding-Personalization-Efforts-in-the-Hospitality-and-Retail-Industries-Report.pdf).

Buehring and O'Mahony interviewed **25 frequent luxury-hotel guests** and identified value in being acknowledged and remembered. Use this as a qualitative rationale for recognition in luxury service; it does not measure hotel retention or profit. [Author manuscript](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/92274/1/JHTI_Memorable_Experiences_Buehring_OMahony_Apr_2019.pdf).

**Calling someone by name is a possible rapport cue, not a requirement for personalization.** A 2005 hospitality article argues for training name and face recall, but it is a conceptual/practice argument rather than a controlled hotel outcome study. More recent evidence is mixed: three behavioral experiments in hospitality/service settings found that name reminders can reduce perceived control and increase customers' interest in customizing; a 2026 frontline-service field experiment and online experiments found name use could improve satisfaction and upselling when it built rapport, but could backfire through discomfort when overused, misused, or used in embarrassing contexts. The newer field evidence is not hotel-specific. So recognition may feel good when it is accurate, natural, and connected to useful service; repeated name use by itself is not a reliable value proposition. [Magnini & Honeycutt (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0010880404270881) · [Name reminders study (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2022.103425) · [Name-use field and online experiments (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2026.03.008).

## How personalization could improve experience and support repeat choice

For this project, personalization is the **use of relevant guest information to adapt a service to that guest's needs or preferences**. A profile, a name greeting, or a stored preference is an input; personalization happens when staff or a process uses it to change the service in a way the guest finds relevant. The proposed mechanism is:

> Accurate, current guest context → a timely service adaptation that fits the guest → less effort or better functional/emotional value, and a signal of care → better service evaluation, trust, or satisfaction → a stronger reason to choose the property again when another stay is feasible.

The evidence supports parts of this chain with different strengths; it does **not** establish the full causal path through to more repeat stays.

| Evidence | What it supports | What it does not show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lei et al. (2024), “Recipe for perceived personalization in hotels.”** Three sequential studies: hotel-manager interviews; a randomized online scenario experiment (**n=919**) imagining a five-star hotel; and a survey (**n=214**). The experiment found that higher service effort and surprise increased perceived personalization. In the survey model, perceived personalization was associated with willingness to pay more (**standardized β=.601, p<.001**) and willingness to share a profile (**β=.430, p<.001**). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2023.104818). | Guests respond to service they perceive as specifically relevant, and effort or an unexpected, fitting gesture can make that relevance more visible. | Experiment outcomes were perceived-personalization ratings in imagined scenarios. The willingness-to-pay/profile findings were self-reported intentions, not transactions or repeat bookings. Surprise is not a requirement: an unexpected but irrelevant gesture is not good personalization. |
| **Bonaretti et al. (2020), “What can I(S) do for you?”** Longitudinal records of **118,647 reservations** across **seven three- and four-star Swiss hotels over 26 months**; **7,756** reservations included a personalization request, of which **4,197 (54.11%)** used an IT-enabled customer-service system. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.im.2020.103346) · [publisher abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378720620302846). | Structured preference capture can make it easier to learn what a guest wants and give staff a usable representation to act on. | This is observational hotel-system data, not a randomized test of personalization on satisfaction or retention. A related, publicly available author manuscript using the seven-hotel data adds an important operational detail: system use alone had no significant association with value ratings in a 2,497-review analysis; among 343 requested-personalization stays with fulfillment data, a higher personalization delivery percentage (fulfilled items / requested items) was associated with better value ratings (adjusted odds ratio 2.37 for a full-scale increase, *p*=.031). This is a selected observational subset, not proof of causation. The authors overlap with the 2017 Piccoli study, so this is not an independent replication. [Related author manuscript, pp. 20–23](https://dds.cct.lsu.edu/ddslab/pdf/luietal2017.pdf). |
| **Piccoli et al. (2017), “The impact of IT-enabled customer service systems…”** One 122-room four-star hotel with a stated **60% business / 40% leisure** mix. MyPage users had higher adjusted odds of better Booking.com service (**OR 1.211**) and value (**OR 1.198**) rating categories. For guests who returned, the distinct initial-OTA and initial-direct booking analyses found more favorable second-stay direct-channel shifts among MyPage users. [Author manuscript](https://dds.cct.lsu.edu/ddslab/pdf/piccoliLuiGruen.pdf#page=8). | Tailored service can be associated with higher service/value evaluations, and the hotel case suggests a possible route to more direct bookings on a later stay. | Use was voluntary, ratings are not randomized, business outcomes were not separated by trip purpose, and the booking-channel models condition on already returning. They do not measure whether personalization causes guests to return. |
| **Hu et al. (2019), “Evolving customer expectations…”** Traces reviewers across repeat stays using **521,083 TripAdvisor reviews at 7,031 hotels** over more than a decade. It reports actual repeat-review behavior; service quality and location matter in re-patronage, and business travelers were more likely than leisure travelers to return to the same hotel in this review sample. [DOI and author record](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2019.04.010). | Provides context on what may make a return stay attractive and supports investigating business travelers as a subgroup. | It does not study personalization. Reviewers are a selected subset of guests, and a repeat review is an imperfect record of all repeat stays. It does not show why business travelers returned or establish a luxury-hotel rate. |
| **Yoon, Peck, and Shu (2025), “Increasing Hotel Loyalty Through Psychological Ownership.”** Four studies include a small hotel field intervention. In the reported comparison, **42** guests were assigned a room and **39** chose between two. Guests offered a choice reported higher likelihood of returning (**6.15 vs. 5.57 on a 7-point scale; d=.53**); actual hotel return bookings were not tracked. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/19389655241309634) · [author manuscript](https://rinyoon.com/assets/pdf/yoon-et-al-2025-increasing-hotel-loyalty-through-psychological-ownership.pdf). | Guest participation and control over a service detail may create a feeling of ownership and increase stated loyalty, even where satisfaction changes little. This suggests a possible path alongside satisfaction. | Room choice is customization, not the same as staff using prior guest data. The return outcome is stated intention in a small single-hotel sample, not observed retention. |

### Recommended problem framing

> Hotels may hold guest preferences and service history, but that context may not consistently reach the employee or process that can act on it. Guests may have to repeat needs or receive uneven service across interactions. This project will investigate how hotels turn relevant, current guest context into timely, fulfilled service across the stay, beginning with one service setting such as the lounge.

This is a **problem hypothesis**, not a verified industry-wide failure. Confirm which guest information the selected hotel actually records, which employees can access it, and whether any service gap occurs often enough to matter. Keep the hotel-wide ambition in the framing while using one department and moment to make field research manageable.

Suggested research questions:

1. **Service delivery:** How do hotel teams turn relevant guest context into service that is useful, timely, and reliably fulfilled?
2. **Guest outcome:** What do guests say that service improves—convenience, comfort, time, continuity, issue resolution, or feeling understood—and when does it feel inaccurate or intrusive?
3. **Repeat choice:** When guests have a real opportunity to choose among hotels, does a relevant, fulfilled service adaptation affect their choice to return to the same property or brand?

Treat the repeat-choice question as a later hypothesis. First establish that personalization is actually being delivered and that guests value the result. A recorded preference or staff greeting a guest by name is not, on its own, evidence of improved experience.

### Interview guide for hotel discovery

Start with a recent event and invite a walk-through. Avoid asking whether people “like personalization” or whether it “would make them loyal”; those prompts invite agreement without showing what happened.

**Hotel leaders, guest-experience teams, and data/CRM staff**

1. “When you say a guest experience is personalized at this property, what would I see staff do differently? Can you give me a recent example?”
2. “Which guest details or preferences are recorded today, where do they come from, and how do you know they are still accurate?”
3. “At what points in the stay and across which departments can staff use that information? Where does it fail to carry across a handover?”
4. “How does the hotel decide which details are useful and appropriate to act on? How can a guest correct or update them?”
5. “How do you know whether a request was actually fulfilled, and who owns the follow-up if it was not?”
6. “What evidence do you currently use to say personalization improved the guest experience or business results? What is the measure and denominator?”
7. “For business travelers, who typically chooses and pays for the hotel? What role do company policy, corporate rates, booking tools, and travel arrangers play?”

**Frontline staff across departments and shifts**

1. “Think of the most recent time information about a guest changed what you did. What did you know, where did you find it, and what did you change?”
2. “Think of a recent time you needed guest context but could not find it, did not trust it, or received it too late. What happened next?”
3. “What information do you ask guests to repeat? How often does that happen in a typical shift, and what does it cost in time or service quality?”
4. “At what point would you need the information to make use of it? Which role or team should receive it?”
5. “What stops you from acting: missing or outdated information, access, uncertainty about what it means, authority, time, or lack of service capacity?”
6. “How do you tell whether an adaptation helped the guest? Have you seen an example where remembering or using a detail felt wrong or intrusive?”
7. “What existing log, checklist, handover note, or service-recovery record could help us count these cases?”

**Returning guests, including business travelers**

1. “Think about your last two trips to this area. Which hotels did you consider, which did you stay at, and who made the booking decision?”
2. “What made the same hotel a workable choice again—or led you to choose another one? How did location, employer rules, price, availability, and the previous stay matter?”
3. “Did a hotel remember or use something from an earlier stay? What did it do, and did that save effort or improve comfort?”
4. “What have you had to explain more than once? What happened when staff did not know something you expected them to know?”
5. “When does a hotel remembering a detail feel helpful, and when could it feel inaccurate, awkward, or too personal?”
6. “What would have to be true for you to choose the same property next time, if you had a genuine choice?”

Ask for examples before asking for general ratings. Include guests who returned and guests who changed hotels; only interviewing loyal guests would hide why some people leave.

### Hotel statistics and data to reques

Ask for aggregate or de-identified data. For every figure, request the **time period, numerator, denominator, definition, data source, and available segment splits**. A useful first request is a recent 12–24-month baseline, if the hotel can provide it.

| What to request | Example measure | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guest and trip mix | Business, leisure, and meetings/events stays; corporate-account share; rate-plan and booking-channel mix | Confirms whether the target segment and workflow exist at useful scale. |
| Repeat behavior | Same-property repeat stays and same-brand returns reported separately; return interval; repeat definition used by the hotel | Avoids blending property loyalty with chain loyalty. Ask whether the data can identify guests who revisited the destination and had a real choice; most PMS data may not show that opportunity. |
| Context coverage and quality | Share of eligible stays/interactions with guest-declared, current, actionable preferences; source and last-confirmed date; incorrect or disputed details | Tests whether useful information exists and whether staff can trust it. Separate guest-stated preferences from inferred information. |
| Availability to staff | Share surfaced to the right role before the service decision; successful retrieval rate; median and 90th-percentile lookup time; handover completion | Measures the proposed access and timing problem. If system logs do not exist, use a short observation sample. |
| Service fulfillment | Requests fulfilled on time / requests due; partial, late, or failed fulfillment; reason for failure; repeat questions | Measures whether the hotel acts on the information. One useful ratio is **personalization delivery percentage (PDC) = requested items fulfilled ÷ requested items**. Define what counts as an item and whether partial fulfillment is allowed. |
| Guest response | Brief post-interaction ratings for relevance, effort saved, comfort/convenience, satisfaction, and privacy comfort; related complaints or service-recovery outcomes | Checks whether staff action was actually perceived as useful and detects backfire. Do not use staff action alone as a proxy for guest value. |
| Commercial outcomes, later | Actual return stays among a return-opportunity cohort; direct-vs-OTA channel among returners; contribution after distribution commission and incremental service cost | Tests whether experience improvements relate to repeat choice or economics. Room revenue, direct bookings, and stated loyalty alone are not profit. |

For business travelers, also request the share of bookings constrained by corporate programs, negotiated rates, travel arrangers, or company booking tools. A rebooking outcome should separate guests with a feasible return trip and booking authority from those who did not control the choice.

## What to do nex

1. **Choose a property and sample:** Prefer a hotel whose service model matches the intended upscale/luxury market. Interview a mix of managers, frontline staff from different shifts/departments, and guests. Treat frequent business travelers as one useful subgroup, not the only customer type.
2. **Map one real guest journey moment:** Ask teams to trace the information from its source through access, interpretation, action, handover, fulfillment, and guest response. Lounge can be the first setting, while the problem statement remains hotel-wide.
3. **Get a baseline before proposing a solution:** Start with preference coverage, lookup/access timing, repeated questions, request fulfillment, and a brief guest-relevance measure. Use observations if the hotel does not log the events.
4. **Decide whether the problem is worth solving:** Check how often it occurs, what it costs guests and staff, what fails today, and whether the hotel can act on the information. If information is already available and reliably used, or there are too few relevant cases, revise the problem before choosing a tool.
5. **Study repeat choice only after that:** Follow actual same-property and same-brand returns over a suitable period, and account for location, rate, availability, trip purpose, corporate restrictions, loyalty status, and prior service. For any causal claim, use a credible comparison or phased rollout. Evaluate financial value as incremental contribution after service, distribution, and technology costs.
