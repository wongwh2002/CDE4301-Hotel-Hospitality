# Additional evidence on hotel personalization, repeat choice, and business travel

**Research date:** 2026-10-16
**Scope:** Additional primary or author/institutional sources beyond the project’s current notes on Piccoli et al. (2017), Bonaretti et al. (2020), Hu et al. (2019), Lei et al. (2024), Kim et al. (2020), Yoon et al. (2025), and the BCD/Medallia surveys. The existing studies are referenced only where a new comparison or interpretation helps.

## What the additional evidence says

There is a plausible link between guest-tailored service and stronger loyalty or willingness-to-pay **intentions**, including in luxury-hotel samples. The direct evidence is still mostly surveys, self-reported intentions, or small experiments that test guest choice rather than hotel staff using stored guest data. The sources reviewed here do **not** establish that personalization raises realized hotel profit, GOP margin, or actual repeat-stay rates.

For business travel, repeat visits to a destination may create a chance to earn repeat hotel business, but the traveler is not always the buyer and may have little freedom to choose. Upscale-hotel studies suggest relationship-building matters more to business than leisure travelers, while choice studies and current corporate-travel data show that value, price, location, and company rules remain substantial forces.

## New evidence most relevant to luxury hotels

### Luxury personalization, loyalty intention, and stated willingness to pay more

Baloglu and Bai’s EuroCHRIE 2024 conference paper examined personalization outcomes among **220 U.S. consumers aged 25–54 who had recently stayed at a U.S. luxury hotel**. The authors used a Qualtrics panel and a cross-sectional structural equation model. Personalization was measured with items about customized services, special treatment, unique offers, and occasion gifts. On seven-point self-report measures, perceived personalization was associated with lifestyle congruence (**standardized β=.64, p<.001**), loyalty intentions (**β=.34, p<.001**), and willingness-to-pay-more (WTPM) (**β=.35, p<.001**). Lifestyle congruence was associated with loyalty (**β=.53, p<.001**) and WTPM (**β=.19, p<.05**); loyalty was also associated with WTPM (**β=.24, p<.05**). The sample’s mean WTPM score was **5.88/7**.

This is a useful, luxury-specific association study, but it is a **conference paper**, not an established journal article. The survey did not observe a booking, a real price premium, or profit. Cross-sectional SEM cannot establish that personalization caused loyalty or willingness to pay; common self-report measures and the restricted age range also limit generalization. The outcome should be described as stated willingness to pay more—not a realized premium or margin uplift. [Baloglu & Bai, *An Empirical Study of the Outcomes of Luxury Hotel Personalization* (EuroCHRIE 2024 proceedings, paper begins p. 180)](https://eurochrie.org/2024/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/EuroCHRIE-2024-Conference-Proceedings.pdf)

### A larger luxury-consumer study shows mixed paths

Dennis Baloglu’s 2025 UNLV dissertation analyzed a Qualtrics sample of **476 U.S. luxury-hotel consumers**. It reports positive paths from perceived personalization to brand-engagement experience and brand-identification connection. Engagement partly mediated the relationship between personalization and behavioral-loyalty **intentions**. But brand identification did **not** significantly lead to behavioral-loyalty intentions, and engagement did not significantly lead to willingness to disclose information. The author also reports that SEM direct effects and machine-learning feature importance diverged for loyalty intentions.

This supports the possibility that tailored experiences can make a brand feel more engaging, while warning that engagement, data disclosure, and loyalty are not interchangeable outcomes. It is a dissertation rather than a peer-reviewed journal article, uses survey constructs rather than observed bookings, and its full text is embargoed until 2030; the institutional record provides the abstract only. [UNLV institutional record and abstract](https://oasis.library.unlv.edu/thesesdissertations/5367/)

### Upscale business and leisure travelers may respond to different relationship strategies

Liu et al. (2015) compared **326 leisure travelers and 282 business travelers** surveyed at upscale hotels in Taipei, including internationally known four- and five-star properties. Their cross-sectional structural-equation analysis found that **brand equity was the leading loyalty factor for leisure travelers, while relationship equity was the leading factor for business travelers**. This makes relationship continuity a reasonable hypothesis to investigate for repeat business stays.

The study does not isolate personalization from other relationship strategies, and it measures survey-modelled loyalty rather than subsequent hotel bookings. Its respondents and destination are not a representative estimate for all luxury business travelers. Treat “relationship equity matters for business travelers” as segment-specific evidence, not proof that recognizing a guest or retrieving a profile causes repeat choice. [Liu et al., *Tourism Management Perspectives* (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tmp.2015.07.017)

A five-star hotel study in Jordan provides a useful comparison: among **451 Arab luxury-hotel consumers**, financial, social, and structural relationship bonds were each associated with attitudinal and behavioral loyalty measures. The study did not find one bond type to be significantly more important than the others. In other words, a personal relationship is one possible part of loyalty-building, alongside financial value and useful service structures. These were survey associations, not observed repeat purchases or profit. [Shammout, *International Journal of Hospitality & Tourism Administration* (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1080/15256480.2018.1464420)

## Repeat travel is not the same as freedom to choose

The project’s existing BCD survey already reports that many business travelers reuse a hotel when revisiting a destination, while location, employer policy, and price outrank past experience in stated hotel-choice factors. The following newer official survey adds evidence about who controls the booking and current cost pressure.

Deloitte’s July 2025 U.S. Corporate Travel Study surveyed **more than 150 travel managers** and **more than 1,000 corporate travelers**. Among frequent business travelers, **49% said they always use corporate booking channels**, up from 43% in 2024. **54% of travel managers** named cost among the top three factors restricting travel; this rose to **64%** among managers at larger companies. This is not a hotel-personalization study, but it reinforces that company rules, booking tools, and budgets shape the traveler’s available options. [Deloitte, *2025 Corporate Travel Study*](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/transportation/corporate-business-travel-survey/2025.html)

Price sensitivity also appears in hotel-choice evidence beyond budget properties. Tanford, Raab, and Kim’s online survey compared **535 U.S. adults** who had stayed in hotels at least twice in the prior year. Price was the most important purchase factor for limited-service guests; **both price and utility mattered for full-service guests**. Limited-service guests were more susceptible to competitors’ discounts and had lower switching costs. Full-service is not synonymous with luxury, and the study was not an experiment or a behavioral booking analysis, but it challenges any assumption that an upscale or business guest simply ignores price. [Tanford, Raab & Kim, *International Journal of Hospitality Management* (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2011.04.006)

This aligns with, rather than replaces, the project’s existing choice-based conjoint finding from Kim et al. (2020): business travelers were generally price-sensitive, with heterogeneous preferences across traveler segments. The practical hypothesis is narrower: when location, company policy, rate, availability, and trip fit are acceptable, a reliable prior experience or useful relationship may help a hotel win the repeat choice.

## Do service and satisfaction translate into actual return?

The project’s existing longitudinal review study by Hu et al. (2019) is particularly useful when interpreted as a warning against using satisfaction as a stand-in for repeat bookings. Across more than **500,000 TripAdvisor reviews at 7,031 hotels**, it traced reviewers who returned to the same property. Service quality became more important to satisfaction as visits accumulated, but hotel location—despite having little weight in overall satisfaction—was an important factor in actual re-patronage. It therefore shows that satisfaction drivers and return drivers can differ. This is observed repeat-review behavior, not all guests’ retention; reviewers are a selected population, and it does not test personalization.

Worsfold et al. (2016) analyzed a large guest-and-employee survey from one global hotel chain using structural-equation modeling. Satisfaction with the **physical attributes** of the hotel had a stronger link to intention to return than satisfaction with service; service satisfaction was associated with perceived value, but perceived value did not significantly predict return intention in their model. This cautions against assuming that a more personalized interaction can compensate for a poor property, location, room, or other basic attribute. It remains one-chain, cross-sectional evidence and measures intention rather than actual return. [Worsfold et al., *International Journal of Contemporary Hospitality Management* (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1108/IJCHM-04-2015-0195)

The current project review also includes Yoon, Peck, and Shu’s 2025 hotel field experiment: guests given a choice between two rooms reported greater psychological ownership and higher stated likelihood of returning than guests assigned a room. The additional interpretation is that **participation and control** may be one reason a tailored experience feels personally meaningful. That study did not test staff using a guest profile, and actual future bookings were not tracked; it should not be presented as evidence for a data-retrieval system. [Yoon et al., *Cornell Hospitality Quarterly* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1177/19389655241309634) · [Author manuscript](https://rinyoon.com/assets/pdf/yoon-et-al-2025-increasing-hotel-loyalty-through-psychological-ownership.pdf)

## Personalization can be mistimed or feel wrong

A 2026 *Tourism Management* study analyzed online hotel-review responses from more than **1,000 TripAdvisor hotels**. Here, “personalization” meant matching a management reply’s topic to the guest’s review, not tailoring in-stay service. Response length, linguistic-style matching, and positive sentiment were associated with higher subsequent retention in the study’s review-based measure. Topic personalization had a **negative association**, especially for dissatisfied reviewers. This is not evidence that in-person hotel personalization is harmful; it is a boundary case showing that a personalized gesture can misfire, particularly when it does not resolve the guest’s underlying problem. The outcome is inferred from review behavior, and the design is observational. [Lahsini, Ranjan & Gorge, *Tourism Management* (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2025.105243)

## Evidence ladder: what can and cannot be claimed

| Claim | Best evidence in this set | What it supports | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guest-tailored experience may increase perceived fit and loyalty intent in luxury hotels. | Baloglu & Bai: 220 recent luxury-hotel guests; personalization associated with loyalty intention (**β=.34**) and WTPM (**β=.35**). | A luxury-specific hypothesis worth testing. | Causality, actual repeat bookings, actual price premium, or profit. |
| Business travelers may respond to relationship-building. | Liu et al.: 282 business and 326 leisure upscale-hotel guests; relationship equity was the leading modeled loyalty factor for business respondents. | Segment-specific relevance of relationship strategies. | That personalization alone is the effective strategy or that it caused observed return. |
| A hotel’s prior service experience may help earn a repeat visit. | Hu et al.: reviewers traced across visits; service quality and location relate differently to satisfaction and re-patronage. | Measure actual returns separately from satisfaction. | That personalization caused the return or that review behavior represents all guests. |
| Guest control can make an experience feel more personally owned. | Yoon et al.: small one-hotel field experiment; two-room choice increased stated return likelihood. | A possible psychological mechanism. | Stored-profile personalization or realized retention. |
| Business travelers may still be constrained by cost and policy. | Deloitte (2025), Tanford et al. (2012), and the project’s existing BCD/Kim evidence. | Choice context and segmentation matter. | That price or policy explains every return decision. |

Across these sources, “profit” remains an unmeasured downstream outcome. A higher loyalty-intention score or stated WTPM score is not revenue, contribution, or GOP. For a hotel-specific financial claim, the property would need actual booking and stay outcomes, a credible comparison group or phased rollout, and the cost of delivering the service.

## How to use this evidence in the project

An evidence-safe framing is:

> **Personalized service may help high-end hotels make repeat experiences feel relevant and dependable. Upscale-hotel studies suggest that relationship strategies can matter to business travelers, and luxury-consumer surveys associate perceived personalization with loyalty and stated willingness to pay more. However, price, location, company booking policy, and basic service quality remain important, and direct evidence of actual repeat bookings or profit from staff using guest data is limited. The hotel should first establish whether relevant guest context is available to frontline staff in time and whether acting on it improves the experience.**

For hotel interviews and any later measurement, separate these stages:

1. **Context availability:** What guest preferences, current issues, or service history are recorded, and who is authorized to see them?
2. **Operational use:** Can the relevant employee access accurate context during the service moment, and does it change what they do?
3. **Guest response:** Do guests report that the service was relevant, easier, or more consistent? Does it avoid making them repeat a request?
4. **Observed choice:** Among guests who actually return to the same destination and had a genuine choice, do they book the same property or brand again? Separate this from satisfaction and from direct-versus-OTA channel choice.
5. **Economics:** What incremental contribution remains after benefit cost, staffing time, commissions, technology, and any displaced paid service or upgrade?

For repeat business travelers, record trip purpose and destination, employer or account rules, available hotel alternatives, rate paid, booking channel, availability, prior visit count, loyalty membership, service issues, and whether the hotel had the guest context before the interaction. Without those variables, a return may be incorrectly attributed to personalization when location, corporate policy, rate, or limited supply explains it better.
