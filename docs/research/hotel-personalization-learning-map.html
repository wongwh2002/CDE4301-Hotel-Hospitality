01 · Current recommendation

Define the service gap first

The project can aim across the hotel while researching one real workflow deeply. A staffed lounge is a practical starting point, not yet the proven highest-value department.

Working problem statement

At hotels that record guest preferences and service history, frontline staff may not consistently have the relevant, current context when they serve a guest. During busy periods and handovers, reliance on memory, manual profile checks, and informal communication may lead to repeated questions or uneven follow-through. This project will investigate how reliably hotels turn guest context into timely, useful service, beginning with one lounge workflow. Problem hypothesis · to validate at a named property, role, and service moment

What this framing says—and does not say

Scope

Hotel-wide opportunity; lounge is the first bounded case. Each department needs its own workflow evidence.

Target market

Upscale/luxury can be a deliberate initial market choice. The current hotel visits do not establish a luxury-industry prevalence rate.

Business case

Personalisation may improve continuity or reduce effort. Direct effects on retention, revenue, or profit are not yet established.

Technology

Smart glasses and face matching are solution candidates, not part of the problem definition.

02 · How the framing developed

From “edge” to testable workflow

This is the project’s reasoning sequence, not a dated history of decisions. The research timeline below shows when the cited studies appeared.

Starting business lens

Hotels need an edge

Service, location, loyalty, revenue mix, cost control, and operations can all create advantage. “Luxury margins are especially thin” needs property- and segment-specific evidence.

Candidate lever

Personal service

Relevant attention may make a stay easier and more human. Recognition alone is not the same as adapting service to a guest’s need.

Possible segment

Repeat business travel

Trips recur, but location, employer rules, price, and booking authority often constrain the hotel choice. Return travel is an opportunity, not proof of loyalty.

Scope choice

Hotel-wide, lounge first

Keep the ambition general. Use one role and service moment in the lounge to see whether the context-to-service chain actually breaks.

Current question

Can context become action?

Find out what is known, who can use it, when it arrives, what staff do, what the guest experiences, and what measurable outcome follows.

Important correction to the margin premise: a CBRE sample of U.S. full-service hotels reported a 33.8% GOP margin for 2024, down from 34.3% in 2023. This is not net profit, not a luxury-only estimate, and not Singapore data. CBRE’s H2 2025 U.S. outlook describes luxury as the strongest-performing chain scale, while also reporting broad labor and operating-cost pressure. A separate report says luxury-lodge margins rose 54% since 2018, but gives relative growth rather than an absolute margin and covers a narrow segment. These sources do not justify “high-end hotels have thin margins” as a general claim. They support measuring the chosen property’s own cost and contribution.

Performance depends on the brand and period, too. In CBRE’s 2019–2024 U.S. public-brand KPI sample, upper-midscale brands led chain scales in RevPAR growth (2.3% CAGR), while the gap between the top- and bottom-performing luxury brands widened to nearly 7 percentage points. A separate 4,200-hotel fee sample found franchise-fee growth highest in luxury (+6.4%) and upper-upscale (+5.0%), but fee share of revenue highest in upscale and upper-midscale hotels. RevPAR and franchise fees are not net profit; use these figures to avoid assuming a chain-scale label predicts the target property’s economics.

03 · What “personalisation” means here

Recognition is a doorway, not the outcome

The research literature uses overlapping definitions. This operational definition is tailored to the project and lets the team observe the service, rather than count profile fields or greetings.

Guest-context personalisation happens when a frontline employee uses relevant knowledge about an individual guest’s needs, preferences, or circumstances to adapt an interaction or service. Whether the guest experiences that adaptation as a good fit is a separate outcome to measure.

01 · identity

Recognition

Does the employee know who the guest is? A correct name or face match may help, but can also be awkward or wrong.

02 · information

Context access

Can the right role find accurate, current, appropriate information before making a service decision?

03 · service

Adaptation

Does staff action change in a way that addresses the guest’s current need—and can the hotel fulfil it?

04 · guest

Perceived fit

Does the guest find it relevant, helpful, and welcome? Could it feel intrusive, inaccurate, or presumptive?

Personalisation

The hotel or employee uses context to adapt service for this person. Example: staff follows up on an open issue without making the guest explain it again.

Customisation

The guest chooses the service configuration. Example: selecting a room preference or how they want an issue resolved.

Recognition / good service

Recognition signals attention; good service may be excellent but generic. Either can accompany personalisation without proving it happened.

Why being recognised may feel good: an accurate, natural acknowledgement can signal attention and individuality. Evidence suggests a possible rapport effect, but its size and welcome depend on the setting. A name is not a substitute for understanding what would help the guest.

Hotel guest evidence

In interviews with 25 frequent luxury-hotel guests, being acknowledged and remembered stood out as an experience value. A hotel scenario experiment found formality mattered: after a service failure, formal address produced higher satisfaction; after successful service, preferred style varied across people.

Small or scenario-based evidence; not a measure of repeat booking or profit. Buehring & O’Mahony · Fan et al.

Name-use evidence outside hotels

A two-week café field study found a small difference in reported service satisfaction when names rather than order numbers were called out (5.35 vs 5.13 on a seven-point scale). A 2026 restaurant study reported higher satisfaction and special-item upsell acceptance with name exchange, but its spending result was not significant across all four conditions.

These settings do not establish hotel effects. Repeated name use or pairing a name with a sales pitch can reduce comfort or rapport. Lim et al. · Raja et al.

What is known about profit?

The name-use studies do not establish hotel profit. One café sales comparison was explicitly exploratory; the restaurant study measured upsell acceptance and spend, not contribution after labor, food, discount, or technology cost. There is no reliable universal profit estimate for calling a guest by name.

Test recognition, adapted service, guest response, cost, and commercial outcome separately.

05 · Evidence timeline

What the studies support

Studies vary in population, outcome, and method. A survey about preference, an association in hotel records, and a measured booking are different kinds of evidence.

1998
Luxury guests and relationship benefits

Survey of 892 luxury-hotel guests, most frequent and primarily business travelers. Respondents supported personal service, room requests, faster registration, flexible arrival/departure, and serious problem handling.

Stated preference
Not actual repeat bookings or profit. Bowen & Shoemaker

2015
Business and leisure guests may value different loyalty drivers

A survey of 608 guests at upscale hotels in Taipei found relationship equity was the leading modeled loyalty factor for business travelers, while brand equity led for leisure travelers.

Survey association
Relationship equity includes more than personalisation; loyalty was modeled, not observed as repeat bookings. Liu et al.

2017
Guest preferences used in one four-star hotel

Observational study of an IT-enabled preference page. Users had higher odds of better service/value ratings. Among guests who already returned, the system was associated with shifts toward direct website booking.

Hotel field data
One hotel, voluntary use. Does not show that personalisation caused the return. Piccoli et al.

2019
Repeat reviews and frequent luxury guests

Longitudinal analysis of 521,083 reviews across 7,031 hotels links service quality with re-patronage over repeat visits and identifies location as an important return factor. A 25-person luxury guest study describes value in being acknowledged and remembered.

Useful context
Selected reviewers; small purposive interview sample. Service quality is broader than personalisation. Hu et al. · Buehring & O’Mahony

2020
Preference data used across seven Swiss hotels

A longitudinal hotel-network field study examined how a digital customer-service system helped staff elicit and fulfil guest preferences. It supports studying the workflow and customer value, but does not report a profit uplift.

Multi-property field data
Observational study; not evidence for glasses or face recognition. Bonaretti et al.

2020
Business-trip hotel choice varies by segment

Choice-based conjoint research reports that business travelers are generally price-sensitive, while traveler groups differ in their preferences.

Stated choices
Scenario choices, not observed bookings. Kim et al.

2023–24
What hotel guests notice and call personal

In a U.S. guest survey, 65% recalled staff already having stay details at an interaction start; 66% recalled issue follow-up. Only 23% rated their recent stay 9–10 for personalisation. Hotel scenario research found several practices similarly personal, with name greeting not a uniquely strong signal.

Guest reports
Recall and perceived-personalisation ratings, not verified failures or booking behavior. Medallia · Lei et al.

2024
Luxury-hotel guests who perceived personalisation also reported stronger loyalty and price-premium intentions

In a U.S. survey of 220 recent luxury-hotel guests, perceived personalisation was associated with loyalty intention (β=.34) and stated willingness to pay more (β=.35); both associations were statistically significant (p<.001). Average stated willingness-to-pay-more was 5.88/7.

Luxury survey
Conference paper and cross-sectional self-report; not a real premium, booking, causal effect, or profit result. Baloglu & Bai

2025
Business-traveler repeat choice and hotel data access

BCD reports 73% say they reuse the same hotel when revisiting a destination; location, employer policy, and price rank above prior experience. A vendor-sponsored hoteliers’ survey reports data-access and disconnected-system barriers.

Segment lead
Self-report is not a retention rate; data-access survey is not a frontline preference-access measure. BCD · Hapi/Revinate

2025
Business traveler choice is shaped by corporate controls and cost

Deloitte’s U.S. corporate-travel survey found 49% of frequent business travelers always use corporate booking channels, up from 43% in 2024. Cost was among the top three travel restrictions for 54% of travel managers and 64% at larger companies.

Industry survey
U.S. travel policy evidence, not hotel-specific or personalization research. Deloitte

2025
Hotel costs, brand performance, and event economics

CBRE U.S. full-service sample: labor is a major cost; revenue growth lagged expense growth in 2024. Brand performance varies even within luxury, while upper-midscale led RevPAR growth over 2019–24. Group business is material demand, but event contribution changes with property, demand, space, food, staffing, and room displacement.

Industry accounts
U.S. samples are not Singapore estimates. Revenue, RevPAR, and GOP margin are not net profit. CBRE costs · Brand KPIs · CBRE F&B

2025
Room choice and guest control

In one hotel field experiment (81 guests), guests who chose between two rooms reported higher likelihood of returning than guests assigned a room (6.15 vs 5.57 on a 7-point scale). Actual later bookings were not measured.

Return intention
Guest choice / customisation, not staff using prior guest data; single-property, modest sample. Yoon et al.

2026
“Personalised” hotel review responses can backfire

Review and response data from over 1,000 hotels found topic matching between a guest review and a manager reply was negatively associated with review-based retention, especially for dissatisfied reviewers. Other response traits had different associations.

Counterevidence
Observational online-response personalisation; repeat review is a proxy for return and does not test in-stay service. Lahsini et al.

Read the Piccoli result precisely: among initial OTA bookers who already made a second stay, preference-system use was associated with 61.3% higher adjusted odds of booking the hotel website for stay two; among initial direct bookers who returned, it was associated with 32.2% lower odds of using an OTA next time. These are adjusted odds of channel choice within returners—not a 61.3% retention increase, percentage-point lift, or profit margin.

How strong is the loyalty evidence overall? A hospitality meta-analysis of 102 studies and 423 effect sizes found that links weaken as the outcome moves from attitudes to return intentions to observed behavior. Treat intention scores as an early signal, not as retained customers or incremental stays. Tanford (2016).

07 · Problem protocol

Use “5 Whys” as a branching investigation

Do not start from “staff lack data” or force every incident into one root cause. Start from a specific recent event and let evidence decide which branch matters.

Start with an observable event: a guest repeated a request, a known preference was missed, an employee looked up a profile, or a handover did not lead to follow-through. Record what happened without assigning a cause.

What exactly failed for whom, and at what moment?

Separate identity recognition, finding context, understanding it, deciding what to do, fulfilling the service, and the guest noticing value.

wrong personcontext missingcontext laterepeat questionrequest not fulfilledguest did not welcome it

Why did that particular step fail?

Walk the timeline: who knew what, where it was recorded, who needed it, when it was available, what else was happening, and what action followed.

not capturedoutdated or unclearno role accesslookup efforthandover not receivedstaff lacked time / authority / capacity

How often does this happen, and what is the denominator?

Count eligible interactions, successful cases, misses, late cases, and failed fulfilment in a defined place and period. Ask whether guests noticed and what the impact was.

What system, role, process, or constraint explains the pattern?

Check the information source, handover ownership, permissions, shift design, demand peaks, staff coverage, and service limits. A technology gap is only one candidate.

What is the smallest supported problem worth changing?

State the population, moment, failure, evidence, impact, and confidence. If cases are rare or guests do not value a fix, choose another problem before designing a tool.

Evidence caution: the two hotel visits share leads around workload, handovers, manual coordination, and guest information. They do not show how often personalization fails across the industry, nor prove a single common cause.

F&B and returning guests

What it supports: ask how context reaches F&B staff and what happens at peaks and handovers.

PARKROYAL · interest / lead

Lounge and handover

VIP recognition in a club lounge was discussed. Notes also mention a peak-period risk that an earpiece message may be missed. The property, interviewee role, and rate of missed messages need confirmation.

What it supports: inspect a lounge workflow and its access rules.
What it does not show: demand for face matching or that a wearable improves service.

Separate problem candidate

Room readiness

Novotel notes report room-release delays of roughly 30–60 minutes associated with manpower and housekeeping constraints. This has a clearer stated guest wait, but is a different problem from personalising service through guest context.

09 · Business logic

Repeat business is not the same as loyalty

Business travelers may return to an area and sometimes a property. Ask whether the traveler had a real opportunity to choose the hotel again.

73%

of 1,035 BCD survey respondents said they reuse the same hotel when revisiting a destination.

77%

selected location among their top three accommodation-choice factors.

56%

selected employer policy among their top three factors; 84% reported using a company booking tool.

53%

selected price among top three, versus 41% for prior experience. BCD self-report, not observed retention.

The survey sample spans North America, Europe, and Asia Pacific and was not a luxury-hotel sample. Repeat destination travel creates a chance for service continuity; it does not prove that personalisation made the traveler return. For a person who mainly chooses on price—or whose employer chooses—the relevant benefit may be less friction or more reliable service, not loyalty.

What the newer luxury and business-traveler studies add: a survey of 220 recent U.S. luxury-hotel guests associated perceived personalisation with loyalty intention (β=.34) and stated willingness to pay more (β=.35), while a separate upscale-hotel study found relationship equity was a stronger modeled loyalty driver for business than leisure guests. These results make the segment worth investigating; neither observes actual repeat bookings or proves that personalisation causes profit. The luxury study is a conference paper, and the business-traveler study measures relationship equity more broadly than tailored service. Read the methods and limitations.

Why recognise a returning guest?

Recognition can reduce repeated explanations and signal that staff are attentive. It is useful only when accurate, natural, relevant, and welcome. Do not make “calling by name” the project outcome.

What can “return” mean?

Same property, same brand, and same destination are different. Track them separately, and include only guests with a likely return opportunity and meaningful booking choice in a retention analysis.

What would make it commercial?

Measure actual incremental contribution, not only ADR, RevPAR, ratings, direct-channel share, stated willingness to return, or loyalty membership.

Evidence ladder: experience → repeat choice → profit

Closest to service

Service ratings

Piccoli’s preference-system users had better service/value ratings in one hotel. Self-selection limits causal interpretation.

Behavior proxy

Repeat review

Large review datasets can observe another review at a hotel, but miss stays that were not reviewed and do not isolate personalisation’s effect.

Intention

Likelihood to return

Small hotel trials and guest surveys can measure stated intention. They do not count actual future bookings.

Not established

Incremental hotel profit

No cited study proves that frontline data-driven personalisation causes higher hotel net profit after delivery, labor, channel, and technology costs.

Other hotel advantages to consider alongside personalisation

Choice drivers

Location, price, access

Convenient area, rate/value, availability, and employer policy can outweigh remembered service for a business trip.

Hotel operations

Reliable basics

Room readiness, cleanliness, Wi-Fi, breakfast, check-in flexibility, maintenance, and service recovery affect the overall stay.

Revenue mix

Rooms, F&B, events

Rooms are usually the largest revenue source and often the most profit-efficient department; F&B, meetings, and events add revenue but have their own labor, supply, and space costs.

Could meetings and conferences be a stronger project angle?

It is commercially plausible and distinct from VIP recognition. A full-service U.S. hotel mix included 23.5% group demand in CBRE’s 2025 sample; that is a share of demand, not conference profit. CBRE’s first-half 2025 analysis found F&B department margin averaged 29.1%, while banquet revenue fell 7.3% at convention hotels and rose 8.7% at resorts. The difference is a reminder that property, event mix, and local demand matter.

A conference-context problem might be: “Can event, banquet, and AV teams access the organiser’s current setup, schedule, dietary, accessibility, and change requirements at the moment they need to act?” Validate recent event cases, rework, missed changes, organiser feedback, and event contribution. That is a separate workflow, with organisers and corporate accounts as repeat buyers and attendees as service recipients.

Evaluate the whole contract: room block, meeting rental, catering, AV, service charges, labor, food, setup, commissions, and space/room opportunity cost. A revenue stream is not automatically a profitable one.

10 · Field research protocol

Make the next interview about a real event

Choose one property, role, and service moment. Ask people to reconstruct the last case before asking for general opinions.

Bound the case

Name the property, department, role, guest type, time window, and service decision.

Reconstruct the journey

Source → access → interpretation → action → fulfilment → guest response. Include the staff handover.

Count the pattern

Sample recent interactions; record cases where context was missing, late, inaccurate, unused, or useful.

Decide whether to proceed

Require meaningful frequency or severity, guest/staff impact, actionable causes, and a measurable baseline.

Questions for hotel leaders, guest experience, and data teams

Definitions, systems, and business measures
  1. When this property says “personalised service,” what would I actually see an employee do differently? Can you walk me through one recent example?
  2. Which guest details are recorded, where do they come from, and how does the guest or staff confirm that they are current?
  3. Which roles can access each type of information, at what moments, and across which departments or shifts?
  4. What share of eligible service interactions have an actionable preference or open request? How is eligibility defined?
  5. How does the property know a request was fulfilled on time? Which existing logs, handover records, or service-recovery records could we inspect?
  6. What outcome has the hotel measured—guest response, staff time, repeat booking, channel mix, revenue, or contribution? What are the numerator, denominator, period, and comparison?
  7. For business travelers, who selects, pays, and books the hotel? What role do negotiated rates, company policy, booking tools, and travel arrangers play?
Questions for frontline employees, across shifts
  1. Think of the last time knowing something about a guest changed what you did. What did you know, where did you find it, and what changed?
  2. Think of a recent time you needed guest context but could not find it, did not trust it, or received it too late. What happened next?
  3. What do guests have to explain more than once? How often in a shift, and what happens when it is repeated?
  4. At what point would this information have been useful? Which role should receive it, and who currently owns the handover?
  5. What prevents action: accuracy, access, uncertainty, time, authority, supplies, or service capacity? Which one affected the most recent case?
  6. How did the guest respond? Have you seen a remembered detail feel inaccurate, awkward, or too personal?
  7. What does the busy period look like in arrivals, requests, and staff available? What tasks are delayed when context retrieval takes time?
Questions for returning guests and business travelers
  1. Think about your last two trips to this area. Which hotels did you consider, where did you stay, and who made the booking decision?
  2. What made the same hotel a workable choice again—or made you choose another? How did location, employer rules, price, availability, and last stay matter?
  3. Did a hotel use something it knew from an earlier stay? What did staff do, and did it save effort, improve comfort, or make no difference?
  4. What have you had to explain more than once? What happened when staff did not know what you expected them to know?
  5. When does a hotel remembering a detail feel helpful? When might it feel wrong or intrusive?
  6. What would have to be true for you to choose that same property next time, if you had a genuine choice?

Statistics and records to request

Ask for aggregate or de-identified data. For every number, request the period, definition, numerator, denominator, data source, and segment split. A 12–24 month baseline is useful if available; observe a short service sample if logs do not exist.

11 · Economics and decision

Measure contribution, not just revenue

Hotel-wide commercial claims need the property’s own costs and a credible comparison. A benefit that costs little may still be useful; an expensive VIP amenity may not pay back.

What current research says

  • Rooms are typically the largest revenue source and often the most profit-efficient department. RevPAR is still a revenue measure, not profit.
  • In CBRE’s 2024 U.S. sample, total hotel revenue grew 2.3% while expenses above GOP grew 4.1%; labor was the largest operating expense category.
  • A U.S. sample reported brand loyalty fees averaging 2.2% of rooms revenue. That is chain-program fees—not the cost of one VIP guest, lounge visit, or perk.
  • There is no dependable universal public estimate of the net profit from one VIP gesture or personalisation action.

How a local pilot could be evaluated

Incremental contribution = incremental room / F&B / event contribution + attributable cost savings − staff time − benefit and supply cost − channel / commission cost − technology and operating cost

Use a comparison group, phased rollout, or another defensible baseline. Adjust for location, rate, season, availability, trip purpose, employer policy, booking channel, and loyalty status. Do not claim saved cost unless labor was actually redeployed or reduced.

VIP-service caution: loyalty programs can show hotel-level benefits in research, and some guests value recognition, but that does not prove a particular complimentary lounge benefit or technology investment is profitable. Measure marginal staff minutes, food/supply use, capacity, fulfilment, and the guest and business outcome for the specific benefit.

12 · Recommended next move

Test the lounge workflow, keep the problem hotel-wide

Frequent business travelers are a useful subgroup for interviews, not the only user group. The first test should show that the operational problem exists and matters.

Start with one specific service moment

Example: a returning guest with an already recorded, guest-stated preference or current request visits the club lounge. Follow how staff learn who the guest is, find context, decide what matters, act, and hand over any follow-up.

First measures

Context correct and available before the decision; lookup time; repeated explanations; on-time request fulfilment; staff minutes; guest-perceived relevance and comfort. Track identity recognition separately from service adaptation.

Decision gate before choosing a solution

Continue

Recurring or severe cases; guests or staff are meaningfully affected; relevant information exists; a clear and actionable bottleneck appears.

Reframe

The main issue is capacity, ownership, system permissions, profile quality, or service fulfilment rather than identification.

Stop this angle

Few eligible cases, context already reaches staff, guests do not value the adaptation, or the hotel cannot act on it.

Only then

Compare candidate interventions against the diagnosed bottleneck and measure the change against baseline.

Where smart glasses currently sit

They remain one possible interface hypothesis. The current architecture document is explicitly a proposal; live camera delivery on the target glasses, staff field access, tap-in/out behavior, acceptable identity confidence, lounge workflow, and success measures remain open. The project can keep its hotel-wide research framing while these technical questions remain unresolved.
