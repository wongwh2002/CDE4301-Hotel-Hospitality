# Hotel personalization in the moment of service: what the evidence supports

- **Research date:** 2026-10-23
- **Purpose:** Test the problem framing that hotels may hold useful guest context, while frontline staff may not be able to access, share, interpret, or act on it at the relevant service moment.
- **Scope:** Primary hotel studies and hotel-specific evidence on guest information, service continuity, employee knowledge use, and technology in guest encounters. This note does not establish what happens at PARKROYAL.

## Short assessment

The strongest defensible problem is a **gap between guest context and its use in service**. Hotel studies show that customer information can be fragmented across systems or informal records, and that having access to information systems does not by itself ensure that guest requests and updates move between departments or shifts. Recent hotel research also treats applying guest knowledge as a people-and-process task involving interpretation, training, and employee empowerment.

The evidence is uneven and context-specific. One Greek hotel survey documents manual retrieval and sharing barriers, one Albanian hotel case documents incomplete request handovers, and a Ghana interview study identifies behavioral and organizational barriers. A 2025 hotel study offers examples of guest data, preferences, and prior conversations as inputs to personalized service, but does not test whether staff can retrieve them during an encounter or whether a tool improves the interaction.

The evidence does **not** establish that hotel workers broadly forget preferences, that guests commonly repeat information, that personalization is routinely deprioritized, or that looking at a screen necessarily disrupts service. It supports investigating those possibilities in a defined service moment. It does not select facial recognition, smart glasses, emojis, or any other interface as the solution.

## Primary evidence

### 1. Guest information can be collected without being easy to access across systems

Sigala’s hotel CRM study surveyed **84 hotels in Greece** and followed up with **five telephone interviews**. The survey hotels were recruited from a directory and screened for hotels that said they were trying to personalize their offer and build customer relationships; this was therefore a selected, convenience sample, not a current census of hotels. The paper reports low integration between hotel ICT systems and customer databases. It says that information did not transfer seamlessly to other applications or hotel staff, so manual, laborious, time-consuming data entry and retrieval were required. The same study describes guest information being gathered through observations and interactions and stored in business books or files. Respondents reported that informal information was difficult to share across employees, properties, and time; some larger-property interviewees also described reluctance to share information they viewed as personally valuable. ([Sigala, 2005, *International Journal of Hospitality Management*, 24(3), 391–413](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2004.08.008); [author manuscript](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221409705_Customer_Relationship_Management_in_Hotels_Implementing_Customer_Relationship_Management_in_the_Hotel_Sector_Does_IT_always_matter))

**How to use it:** This is direct hotel-specific support for the possibility that information may exist but be hard to retrieve or share. It supports asking where guest context lives and how it reaches staff, rather than assuming a profile screen solves the issue.

**Limits:** The data are from Greece in the early 2000s and a sample selected for interest in personalization. The findings cannot establish the prevalence of these conditions in contemporary hotels, Singapore, or PARKROYAL. The reported reluctance to share was a finding from interviewees, not a universal staff behavior.

### 2. Hotel service systems may be available while handovers still fail

Özdemir, Çolak, and Shmilli mapped front-office processes in a **four-star, 166-room business hotel in Tirana, Albania**, with about 100 employees. The authors say the hotel’s management software linked departments and allowed employees to access customer information. Yet, in their mapped process for handling guest requests and complaints, staff did not prepare shift-end reports for managers, and reception did not receive feedback from housekeeping or maintenance about whether requests had been completed. The paper says reception could not answer guest requirements when it lacked that information; the authors proposed reports and feedback between departments. ([Özdemir, Çolak, & Shmilli, 2019, *Quality & Quantity*, 53, 2305–2322](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-018-0727-4); [institutional full text](https://gcris.agu.edu.tr/bitstreams/980ae729-6f3a-4222-8f24-858e3b0f9320/download))

**How to use it:** This case suggests that the bottleneck can be workflow continuity and feedback, even where a hotel has connected systems. It gives the report a concrete example of why “the information is in the system” and “the employee can use it at the service moment” are different claims.

**Limits:** This is a single-property process case. It concerns requests such as repairs, amenities, and information—not guest preferences or personalization—and does not report guests repeating requests. Do not generalize its workflow to other hotels.

### 3. Personalization can draw on preferences and prior conversations, but those are capability indicators

Hiezl and Gyurácz-Németh developed a hotel personalization-supporting-technology measure using an expert panel of **11 specialists** and validation interviews with **10 hotel general managers** (21 interview participants across those stages) to refine 26 indicators, then surveyed **105 hotels** from a population of 458 (about a 23% response rate). Thirty-one respondents were chain hotels, and represented properties ranged from 10 to 429 rooms. The scale includes examples such as storing and updating guest data, providing information or products according to preferences, recommending activities based on a previous guest conversation, and anticipating a guest’s wants based on prior guest data. Their analysis found variation among participating hotels and more uptake of high-touch, employee-supported practices than some high-tech features. ([Hiezl & Gyurácz-Németh, 2025, *Strategic Change*, 34(4), 609–630](https://doi.org/10.1002/jsc.2663); [full text and measures](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jsc.2663))

**How to use it:** This is a useful research-grounded source for defining what “personalization” might involve: using guest preferences, previous conversations, or stored context to shape recommendations and service. It also supports framing technology as potentially supportive of human service.

**Limits:** These examples are scale items, not evidence that every surveyed hotel stores or uses each type of information. The study relies on a small, self-reported hotel sample and measures provider capability, not guest outcomes or staff retrieval time during live service. It does not validate using names, allergies, coffee preferences, or meeting schedules as a particular display set.

### 4. Applying guest knowledge is also a staff and organizational process

Wavei and Lo’s ahead-of-print 2026 study used in-depth interviews with **30 managers and frontline employees at three- to five-star hotels in Ghana**. It examines how hotel employees generate, share, interpret, and apply customer knowledge. The authors identify employee training, empowerment, and knowledge translation as factors that can help employees mobilize customer knowledge in service encounters; they identify bureaucracy, knowledge hoarding, limited resources, and implementation uncertainty as barriers. ([Wavei & Lo, 2026, *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Insights*, ahead of print, October 1, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/JHTI-01-2026-0070); [PolyU research record and abstract](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/handle/10397/121735))

**How to use it:** This supports treating the problem as more than a search-interface question. Staff need relevant information to be shared, understood, and usable within their role and workflow.

**Limits:** The evidence is qualitative and specific to 30 participants in Ghana. It identifies mechanisms and barriers; it does not estimate their prevalence or establish that they occur at PARKROYAL.

## Why recognition and rapport matter

Buehring and O’Mahony’s luxury-hotel study combined a Delphi exercise with hotel experts and in-depth interviews with frequent luxury-hotel guests. Guests’ most significant reported finding was the value of being acknowledged and personally recognized as unique individuals, which differed from some hosts’ ideas of what counted as personalization. The authors note that the sample was relatively small and that guests were recalling past stays, so this is exploratory guest evidence rather than a quantified estimate of how much recognition changes satisfaction or loyalty. ([Buehring & O’Mahony, 2019, *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Insights*, 2(4), 358–376](https://doi.org/10.1108/JHTI-11-2018-0077); [author manuscript](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/92274/1/JHTI_Memorable_Experiences_Buehring_OMahony_Apr_2019.pdf))

The useful concept behind the “a friend would remember me” analogy is **professional rapport**, not friendship. Gremler and Gwinner describe rapport as an enjoyable service interaction with a personal connection; across two service contexts, their study found positive relationships between rapport and satisfaction, loyalty intent, and word of mouth. The paper is not hotel-specific, and a service relationship does not need to resemble a personal friendship. ([Gremler & Gwinner, 2000, *Journal of Service Research*, 3(1), 82–104](https://doi.org/10.1177/109467050031006))

Using a guest’s name can be one possible recognition cue, but it should not become the definition or success measure of personalization. Srinivasa Raja, Cheng, and Danaher’s 2026 *Journal of Retailing* article, using a real-world field experiment on sales and three online experiments, reports that employee-initiated name use can improve satisfaction and upselling effectiveness through rapport, but can reduce satisfaction when overused, misused, or used in embarrassing contexts. This is not a hotel study; it is a useful warning to test whether name use feels natural in the target interaction. ([Srinivasa Raja et al., 2026, “Call me by my name: The effects of addressing customers by their names, the underlying mechanisms, and the boundary conditions,” *Journal of Retailing*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2026.03.008))

**Report implication:** Explain that personalization matters because guests can value feeling recognized as an individual and because rapport is associated with positive service evaluations. Avoid saying that guests are “like friends” or that calling someone by name automatically creates rapport. A relevant preference or remembered request may be a more useful service outcome than name recognition alone.

## Does technology itself interrupt or weaken the interaction?

Giebelhausen, Robinson, Sirianni, and Brady’s peer-reviewed study combined two analyses of J.D. Power hotel guest-satisfaction data with a follow-up experiment. It found that technology’s effect on service evaluations depended on whether the employee was building rapport: technology could impede a guest’s response to rapport-building, but could also provide a useful way to disengage from an unpleasant interaction when rapport was absent. This is evidence that technology can alter the interpersonal exchange, not evidence that every glance at a screen is harmful. ([Giebelhausen et al., 2014, “Touch Versus Tech,” *Journal of Marketing*, 78(4), 113–124](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/2110a30d-76af-4ebf-9bf5-82072a90215a))

A Cornell Hospitality Research report adds a hotel field experiment in which guests using a lobby touchscreen near a bell stand preferred interacting with the touchscreen while an employee was nearby but not directly engaging them. This illustrates that technology can coexist with human service when the interaction is designed around the guest’s moment. ([Giebelhausen, 2014, “Cyborg Service: The Unexpected Effect of Technology in the Employee–Guest Exchange”](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/50c34d43-22dd-48f7-9c78-3c16e75c38b3))

As a further counterpoint, a survey study at one U.S. luxury hotel brand found employees perceived work mobile devices as relevant and helpful to their job performance. It measured employee perceptions, not objective performance or the effect of retrieving guest profiles in a face-to-face interaction. ([Jeong, Lee, & Nagesvaran, 2016, *International Journal of Hospitality Management*, 57, 40–51](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2016.05.003))

**Implication:** Avoid writing that screens or colleague consultation inherently interrupt good service. Treat interaction flow as a design criterion to test: whether the cue is timely, unobtrusive, relevant, and compatible with staff–guest rapport.

## Claim check

| Proposed claim | What the evidence establishes | Safe report wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hotels collect guest context | Hotel studies document data capture through systems and staff–guest interactions; a recent hotel measure includes preferences, updated guest data, and prior conversation as personalization inputs. | “Hotels may collect guest context that can support personalized service.” |
| Staff cannot access that context | A selected Greek hotel sample reported integration and retrieval problems; other hotel research documents sharing and handover barriers in particular cases. | “Hotel studies identify access and continuity problems in some contexts; whether they occur in our selected workflow needs local validation.” |
| Staff rely on memory and individual familiarity | Some hotel research describes informal knowledge and uneven sharing, but does not establish universal dependence on personal memory. | “Context may sit in formal records, informal notes, or staff knowledge; map where it is in the target workflow.” |
| Guests have to repeat themselves | The sources above do not directly measure repeated guest explanations caused by missing personalization context. | Treat as an interview or observation question, not a stated industry fact. |
| Screen or colleague lookup disrupts rapport | Service-technology studies show effects vary by rapport and interaction design; they do not examine staff looking up a guest profile in a lounge. | “A staff-facing cue should preserve interaction flow; test whether the proposed format does so.” |
| Personalization improves loyalty or satisfaction | The cited sources provide rationale and capability measures, but the evidence summarized here does not prove that this specific intervention improves guest outcomes. | Measure guest-perceived relevance separately from identification accuracy and staff usability. |
| Facial recognition is the appropriate fix | None of these sources establishes this. | Keep identification method and display format as design decisions to evaluate after confirming the service need. |

## Suggested report rationale and HMW

> Hotels may collect guest information that could help staff tailor service, from stated preferences to details shared in earlier conversations. Yet recorded information does not automatically reach the employee who needs it: hotel studies describe fragmented retrieval, informal knowledge that is difficult to share, and service requests that are lost across department or shift handovers. This suggests a gap between knowing about a guest and using relevant context during a live interaction. Whether that gap exists in the selected PARKROYAL lounge workflow—and what kind of support staff and guests would find useful—needs to be established through local observation and testing.

**How might we help hotel frontline staff access and act on relevant, authorized guest context at the right moment, so they can provide attentive service while keeping the guest–staff interaction natural?**

This wording keeps the problem solution-neutral. It does not claim that staff are cognitively overloaded, that guests routinely repeat themselves, or that technology is inherently disruptive. The PARKROYAL lounge can be presented as the setting for validating and testing the question, not as proof that the issue is already confirmed there.

## Questions to validate in the lounge

- What guest context, if any, is currently recorded and available to lounge staff?
- At which service moments would a staff member want to use it, and what action would it change?
- Where do staff currently find the information, and what happens when it is missing, stale, or unclear?
- How do information and unresolved requests move between shifts or departments?
- Would a cue be noticed and trusted without taking attention away from the guest? What timing and display format would work?
- Which details are appropriate and authorized for staff to see, and how can the team avoid treating a correct identity match as proof of personalized service?
