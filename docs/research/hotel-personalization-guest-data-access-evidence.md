# Evidence for hotel personalization and guest-data access

**Research date:** 2026-10-09
**Purpose:** Assess evidence for the exploratory problem statement that hotels may hold useful guest information but frontline staff may not have the right context in a usable form at the moment of service.

## Short assessmen

The evidence supports investigating this problem, while keeping it as a hypothesis. Guest surveys report a gap in how personalized hotel stays feel, including during the stay itself. Hotel professionals also report difficulty accessing data and fragmented systems. In one hotel field study, a new preference-capture pathway was used more often and gathered more preference items than the traditional pathway, though the study does not isolate causation. A recent qualitative study describes customer knowledge spread across systems and employee memory.

These sources do **not** establish that frontline staff at the project properties regularly miss guest context, that access problems cause inconsistent personalization, or that smart glasses or face matching are the right fix. Most useful next step: validate one property, one staff role, and one service moment using recent examples. Ask what information was needed, where it was, how long it took to retrieve, whether staff could act on it, and what happened when it was unavailable.

## Evidence by claim

### 1. Guests report that hotel personalization is limited, especially during the stay

Medallia Market Research surveyed **1,749 hotel guests** about a recent stay in November 2023. Its 2024 report says **23%** rated that stay **9–10 out of 10** for personalization. Across seven personalization elements associated with the stay, average guest recall was **46%**. Recall was higher for booking (52%), check-in (53%), customer-service interactions (63%), check-out (51%), and post-stay contact (53%). The customer-service figure is based only on guests who had a service interaction. The survey question asked guests which listed personalization elements they noticed or experienced during each stage. [Medallia report (PDF)](https://www.medallia.com/wp-content/uploads/pdf/resources/Medallia-Understanding-Personalization-Efforts-in-the-Hospitality-and-Retail-Industries-Report.pdf) · [Medallia release and methodology](https://www.medallia.com/press-release/medallia-research-finds-61-percent-of-consumers-are-willing-to-spend-more-for-personalized-experiences/)

**How to use it:** This is the clearest guest-side evidence that personalization is not consistently noticed, with the on-stay service moment a plausible area to explore. It does not tell us why guests did not notice personalization; it cannot be used as evidence that staff lacked access to a profile or that a data tool would improve the experience.

**Limits:** The survey is self-reported and based on a research panel. The report says results were weighted by U.S. census age and gender; it is not a property-level audit or an observed service study. The figure measures recall of the study’s listed elements, not an objective rate of personalized service.

### 2. Some guests are open to hotels using their data for better service, with consent conditions

Oracle Hospitality and Skift’s *Hospitality in 2025* study surveyed **5,266 consumers and 633 hotel executives** across nine global markets in five languages in spring 2022. Asked about hotels using AI to analyze travel information for better customer service during a trip, **28.7%** were “very interested” and **45.2%** “somewhat interested” if they had willingly provided the data for that purpose—about **74% combined**. Around 19% were not that interested and 7.1% said it would violate their privacy. [Oracle/Skift report (PDF)](https://www.oracle.com/asean/a/ocom/docs/industries/hospitality/hospitality-industry-trends-for-2025.pdf) · [Oracle methodology and release](https://www.oracle.com/news/announcement/oracle-hospitality-in-2025-consumer-research-study-2022-06-01/)

**How to use it:** This supports the narrower point that many surveyed travelers saw potential value in relevant service based on their data, often conditional on voluntarily sharing it. It does not establish that guests want every type of personalization, that they expect staff to recognize them automatically, or that they consent to biometric identification.

**Limits:** The survey is from 2022, measures stated interest in a hypothetical AI use, and is sponsored by a hospitality technology vendor. Guest views are mixed; the privacy qualification matters. Treat it as evidence of conditional openness, not blanket demand.

### 3. Hotel professionals report access and integration problems

Hapi and Revinate’s 2025 *Future of Hotel Data* survey covered **nearly 200 hospitality professionals** at brands, management companies, and properties globally, fielded from April 14 to May 7, 2025. Its release reports that **49%** struggle to access the data they need for critical revenue and operational decisions; **40%** cite disconnected systems as their biggest data obstacle. Other reported barriers include department disconnects (13.77%) and lack of timely data (7.19%). The release also says **19.4%** identified personalization as an area affected by data-accuracy problems. [Survey findings and method](https://www.revinate.com/press-releases/the-future-of-hotel-data-report-reveals-nearly-50-of-hoteliers-struggle-to-access-critical-data/) · [Full report landing page](https://www.revinate.com/the-future-of-hotel-data/)

**How to use it:** This is the most direct industry evidence for the “data exists, but is not easy to access or use” part of the hypothesis. The reported barriers are compatible with data split between systems or teams and information arriving too late to help.

**Limits:** The survey was published by two hospitality technology vendors. The release describes a global professional sample but does not provide the exact respondent count, response rate, role mix, country breakdown, or enough question wording to independently assess sampling and denominators. The 49% figure covers data for broad operational and revenue decisions, not specifically frontline access to guest preferences during service; the results are self-reported and do not establish frequency or cause at a particular hotel.

### 4. A hotel case study connects information design with preference capture and service ratings

Piccoli, Lui, and Grün’s peer-reviewed 2017 *Tourism Management* study used mixed methods at one independent four-star hotel with 122 rooms. It combined staff interviews and observation with **98,330 reservation records from 2010–2014** and 31,269 internal PMS “traces.” The hotel’s traditional process recorded guest requests in the PMS and routed them to departments—for example, a pillow request to housekeeping. The hotel then introduced a guest-facing page with 57 options for expressing preferences. The authors estimated personalization in **13.93%** of reservations using the IT-enabled process, compared with **2.14%** using the traditional process; guests who used the page requested an average of **5.94 items per stay**, versus **1.06** through the traditional process. Among 4,706 linked Booking.com reviews, using IT-enabled personalization was associated with higher service and value ratings after controls. [Article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2016.08.015) · [Open author manuscript](https://dds.cct.lsu.edu/ddslab/pdf/piccoliLuiGruen.pdf)

**How to use it:** This is a useful operational example of a hotel storing guest requests in its PMS and routing them across departments. It also suggests that a clearer, lower-friction way to capture preferences can increase how often and how specifically guests provide them.

**Limits:** It is a single-hotel case and the data are from 2010–2014. The intervention mainly changed **guest preference capture** before arrival; it did not test whether frontline staff could quickly retrieve a profile during an encounter. Guests chose whether to use the page, so the association with service/value ratings is not proof of causation.

### 5. Frontline qualitative research describes knowledge as both recorded and taci

Wavei, Lo, and Gedecho’s 2025 peer-reviewed qualitative case study interviewed **33 people**—14 frontline managers and 19 frontline employees—at three- to five-star full-service hotels in Accra, Ghana. Interviews were purposively and snowball sampled; participants had direct guest contact and at least a year of hotel experience. The study examines knowledge accumulation, formation, retention, and use within customer-relationship practices. Participants described retaining customer and operational knowledge in PMS/CRM systems, shared repositories, and communication tools, while some know-how remained in employees’ memory. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1080/15256480.2025.2549991) · [Publisher full text](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15256480.2025.2549991)

**How to use it:** This supports investigating whether guest context lives in formal records, staff memory, or handovers—and how teams convert it into action. It also cautions against assuming the issue is only a software interface problem.

**Limits:** This is qualitative evidence from a small, selected group of Accra hotel workers. It helps explain possible mechanisms but cannot estimate how prevalent the problem is across hotels. The authors’ broad claims about transferability should be treated cautiously given the one-city sample and the study’s own limitations.

## What the evidence supports—and what it does no

| Proposed claim | Evidence strength | Careful wording |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Guests value relevant, personalized service | Moderate; guest survey reports and stated interest | “Many surveyed guests are open to relevant personalization, subject to context and consent.” |
| Hotels may have guest information but struggle to make it usable | Moderate; global practitioner self-report, plus hotel case studies | “Hotel professionals report data-access and integration barriers; the scale of the issue at our target property is unverified.” |
| Frontline service can depend on data, memory, and team processes | Supporting qualitative and single-property evidence | “Guest context may be distributed across systems and staff knowledge; investigate where it sits in the selected workflow.” |
| Poor access causes inconsistent personalization in the project’s target setting | Not established | Measure recent cases locally before claiming this as a finding. |
| Smart glasses or face matching solve the issue | Not established | Keep the solution open until the underlying access, identification, timing, and action bottleneck is observed. |

## Suggested solution-neutral framing

> Hotels may collect guest information that could support more personal service, but frontline staff may not always have the relevant context in a timely, usable form when serving guests. As a result, personalization may depend on memory, manual profile checks, or handovers and may vary across service moments.

This is still a hypothesis. External evidence justifies investigating it; the project’s hotel visits and follow-up interviews must establish whether it occurs locally, how often, for which staff and moments, and what the actual bottleneck is.
