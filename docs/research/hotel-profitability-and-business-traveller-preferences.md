# Hotel profitability and business-traveller preferences

**Research date:** 2026-10-09
**Purpose:** Find evidence on hotel profit drivers and whether repeat business travellers value personalization, loyalty, or price most when choosing a hotel.

## Short assessmen

There is no single hotel “profit factor.” Rooms are usually the largest revenue source and CBRE describes the rooms department as typically the most profit-efficient. Room performance depends on both the rate charged and the share of rooms sold; profit then depends on what remains after operating costs. Recent U.S. hotel data show why cost control matters: labor is the largest operating expense, and in 2024 expenses rose faster than revenue, putting operating margins under pressure.

The user’s price-first intuition is supported by evidence, including a hotel-choice study that found business travellers generally price-sensitive. At the same time, business travel can be repetitive: one 2025 survey found that many travellers return to the same hotel at a destination and belong to loyalty programs. That is not proof they will pay more for a brand, however. In that same survey, location, employer policy, and price ranked above loyalty; many travellers belonged to multiple programs. Evidence that business travellers value personalization is encouraging but less current and based on stated preferences, not observed spending.

For this project, use personalization as a possible way to make service more relevant and consistent—not as a proven profit lever. The research does not show that personalization increases hotel margins or that smart glasses are the right way to deliver it.

## What drives hotel operating profit?

### Rooms are central, but revenue is not profi

CBRE’s 2025 U.S. hotel analysis says rooms have traditionally generated the most revenue, followed by food and beverage, and that the rooms department is typically the most profit-efficient department. A separate CBRE analysis compared the operating statements of **1,343 full-service U.S. hotels** reporting in both 2019 and 2024. After adjusting for inflation, 2024 GOP was **78% of its 2019 level**, while total revenue and rooms revenue were each **85%** of their inflation-adjusted 2019 levels. These are sample-wide comparisons, not forecasts for a particular property. [CBRE, “Finding Revenue Beyond Rooms and F&B”](https://www.cbre.com/insights/articles/finding-revenue-beyond-rooms-and-f-and-b)

The main room-revenue indicators are average daily rate (ADR) and occupancy. RevPAR—room revenue divided by rooms available—combines both. RevPAR is a top-line measure, though: it does not subtract labor, commissions, utilities, supplies, or other operating costs. [CoStar/STR glossary](https://www.costar.com/products/str-benchmark/resources/glossary)

### Costs can erase revenue growth

CBRE’s preliminary **2,600-hotel U.S. sample** found that in 2024 total hotel revenue grew **2.3%**, expenses above the gross operating profit (GOP) line grew **4.1%**, and expenses below GOP grew **3.6%**. CBRE reports that GOP and EBITDA margins declined in both 2023 and 2024. Salaries, wages, and benefits—the largest operating expense category—rose **4.8%** in the 2024 sample. [CBRE, “All Eyes on Operating Costs in 2025”](https://www.cbre.com/insights/articles/all-eyes-on-operating-costs-in-2025-lessons-learned-in-2024)

As a concrete example, the **2024 U.S. full-service hotel sample** had a GOP margin of **33.8%**, down from **34.3%** in 2023; expenses rose **4.6%** against revenue growth of **3.8%**. Salaries, wages, bonuses, and benefits made up **52% of operating expenses** in that segment. The GOP figure is an operating margin, not the owner’s final net profit after financing and other ownership costs. [CBRE 2025 U.S. Trends sample report (PDF)](https://pip.cbrehotels.com/docs/default-source/samples/trends/annual-trends/2025-annual-us-trends-sample.pdf?sfvrsn=2)

**Implication:** Better occupancy or higher room rates can help, but protecting margin also requires controlling costs. A personalization tool would need a measurable benefit—such as saved staff time, fewer service failures, stronger guest feedback, or incremental revenue—large enough to justify its operating and implementation costs.

## What do business travellers value?

### Price matters, and preferences vary

Kim, Hong, Park, and Kim’s peer-reviewed 2020 study used an online choice-based conjoint survey of **513 hotel customers** considering family-vacation and business-sales-trip scenarios. A latent-class model found different preference segments within both contexts; the authors report that business travellers were **generally price-sensitive**. This supports treating price as a serious constraint, while the segment differences caution against assuming every traveller behaves alike. The results are stated choices in survey scenarios, not observed bookings. [Kim et al., *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Management* (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhtm.2020.08.014)

A 2019 peer-reviewed case study asked customers of a Finnish hotel chain what attributes and possible loyalty-program benefits they wanted. The survey was emailed to **23,518** customers in 2015; **1,315 usable responses** remained after cleaning, a **7.1% response rate**. The authors report that value for money and in-room amenities were top hotel-choice attributes, while reward-program benefits had weak relationships with hotel choice even among frequent travellers. Frequent business travellers tended to accumulate benefits over time; suggested benefits such as upgrades and late checkout could cost less than discounts. This was one Finnish chain’s stated-preference case study, not an experiment showing that a loyalty offer changed actual bookings. [Pesonen, Komppula & Murphy, *Tourism Management* (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2019.01.023) · [Publisher abstract and methods](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261517719300299)

### Repeat travel creates an opportunity, not guaranteed loyalty

BCD Travel Research surveyed **1,035 business travellers** online in **May 2025** across North America, Europe, and Asia Pacific. When asked to select their top three hotel-choice factors, respondents named **location (77%)**, **employer policy (56%)**, and **price (53%)** most often; **previous experience was 41%**, while **hotel brand and loyalty program were each 31%**. **73%** said they repeatedly stay at the same hotel when revisiting a destination. **80%** reported membership in a hotel loyalty program, but **77%** belonged to two or more programs. This is evidence of repeat behaviour and widespread membership, but it does not show that guests are exclusive to one brand or willing to pay a price premium. The survey is self-reported and was conducted by a travel-management company with a commercial interest in business travel. [BCD Travel Research, *Traveler Insights: Hotel Booking Trends* (PDF)](https://www.bcdtravel.com/wp-content/uploads/BCD-report-Traveler-insights-Hotel-booking-trends.pdf)

### Personalization may matter to the experience, but direct evidence is limited

In a **2018** GBTA survey in partnership with Omni Hotels & Resorts, **500 U.S. business travellers** who had stayed in a hotel on their last business trip answered an online survey. **84%** said a personalized guest experience was important, **82%** said loyalty programs mattered in hotel booking, and **81%** believed loyalty membership resulted in better service. These are useful signals of reported attitudes, but the survey is older, self-reported, and sponsored by a hotel chain; it does not show that personalization changed hotel choice, guest outcomes, or profit. [GBTA, “Business Travelers Say Loyalty Matters in Hotel Booking”](https://gbta.org/business-travelers-say-loyalty-matters-in-hotel-booking/)

Related evidence from actual booking records points to experience quality as one factor for business travellers. A 2024 *Journal of Marketing* study combined actual bookings from a major hotel chain’s properties in **six U.S. markets** with online reviews. It found that both a hotel’s and its competitors’ review sentiment affected bookings more for business than leisure travellers; review content about subjective experience attributes also mattered. This supports the importance of delivering a good stay, but it does not test personalization or staff access to guest profiles. [Cho et al., *Journal of Marketing* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429231191449)

### Does personalization make them return to the same property?

The evidence is promising but does **not** yet show that personalization itself causes a repeat stay. Hu et al. (2019) traced the same reviewers' assessments of the same hotels across visits in more than **500,000 TripAdvisor reviews** covering **7,031 hotels** and over ten years. This is stronger than asking guests whether they intend to return: it examines observed repeat reviews as a proxy for actual re-patronage. The authors found that hotel location was an important factor in returning to the same property, and that service quality carried more weight in re-patronage as visits accumulated. Online reviewers are a selected subset of hotel guests, so the results should not be treated as a representative return rate. [Hu et al., *Tourism Management* (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2019.04.010) · [Aalto research record](https://research.aalto.fi/en/publications/evolving-customer-expectations-of-hospitality-services-difference/)

For business travelers specifically, Chan and Wong's survey of **573 frequent individual travelers to Hong Kong** found that location and good service mattered beyond price. Business respondents placed more weight on their previous hotel experience, good service, convenience, and company recommendations. These are reported choice factors, not tracked bookings, and the study is older and destination-specific. It nevertheless supports a practical explanation for repeat choice: a hotel that is well placed for the work trip and has already delivered a dependable stay reduces the risk and effort of choosing again. [Chan and Wong, *Journal of Vacation Marketing* (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1356766706062154)

Personalization may strengthen that dependable experience. In a single four-star hotel, Piccoli, Lui, and Grün (2017) found that guests who used a preference-capture system reported better service and value ratings. Among returning guests whose first booking was through an OTA, first-stay use was associated with higher odds of switching to the hotel website for the second stay. This is evidence about ratings and the **channel used by returning guests**; because the repeat-booking comparison includes guests who came back, it does not show that the system increased the probability of returning. It also does not establish an increase in GOP or net profit. [Piccoli, Lui, and Grün, *Tourism Management* (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2016.08.015)

Luxury-focused evidence helps identify the kinds of personalization that may fit frequent business stays. Bowen and Shoemaker surveyed **892 luxury-hotel guests**, most frequent travelers and primarily business travelers. Respondents supported personalized service, specific-room requests, faster registration, flexible check-in/check-out, upgrades, and employees who take problems seriously. These are stated preferences, not measured repeat bookings. [Bowen and Shoemaker, *Cornell Hotel and Restaurant Administration Quarterly* (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1177/001088049803900104)

Taken together, the research suggests a hierarchy for hotel choice: **fit with the trip** (location and employer policy), **value and dependable basics**, then **a prior experience that makes the property easy and safe to choose again**. Personalization can contribute when it removes friction or helps staff act consistently on relevant preferences—for example, a room request, efficient arrival, a current service issue, or a request that must carry across departments. A greeting by name can signal attention, but it is a thinner benefit than staff using accurate context to improve the stay.

## What this means for the projec

The evidence supports a balanced, hotel-wide framing:

> Business travellers may return to the same destination and sometimes the same hotel, but they still weigh practical constraints such as location, company policy, and price. A hotel may hold guest context that could help staff provide a more consistent experience across arrival, the room, service recovery, meetings, lounge/F&B, and departure. Whether staff can access and use the relevant context in time—and whether guests notice a difference—needs to be tested at the target property. The lounge can be one pilot setting for a hotel-wide problem hypothesis.

This is a stronger basis than saying business travellers are inherently loyal or that personalization directly raises profit. For a lounge proof of concept, validate one role and service moment, but describe the research problem at the hotel level. Ask staff about recent returning-guest cases: what context would have helped, where it was stored, how staff retrieved it, whether it arrived in time, and what happened when it did not. If the team wants to make a retention or financial claim, track actual repeat bookings and compare results while accounting for location, corporate booking rules, price, and service costs.

## Evidence limits

- CBRE profitability data cited here are U.S. hotel samples; the **33.8% GOP margin applies to full-service hotels in CBRE’s 2024 sample**, not all hotels or the project’s properties.
- BCD, GBTA/Omni, and the Finnish-chain study ask travellers about reported or hypothetical preferences. Those measures are not equivalent to observed willingness to pay, repeat booking, or incremental profit.
- The 2020 conjoint and 2024 actual-booking study show that customer segments and research methods matter. “Business traveller” is not a single behaviour type.
- No cited study establishes that on-property personalization by frontline staff causes higher margins, or that face recognition or smart glasses are required.
