# Hotel VIP-service costs and profitability

**Research date:** 2026-10-10
**Purpose:** Find evidence on what hotels pay to serve loyalty/VIP guests and whether those costs are offset by profit.

## Short assessmen

There is no reliable universal figure for the cost or net profit of one VIP guest, lounge visit, or personalization action. “VIP” can mean a loyalty-program member, an elite-tier guest, a guest receiving complimentary benefits, or a guest paying for club-floor access; those categories have different economics.

The best available public figures cover **loyalty-program fees and broad program performance**, not the marginal cost and return of an individual perk. A 2024 U.S. hotel sample found that brand loyalty fees averaged **2.2% of rooms revenue**. Hotels also pay separately to deliver member benefits such as complimentary food and beverage, upgrades, housekeeping services, service-recovery points, and executive-lounge access. Research finds positive hotel-level results for loyalty-program spending overall, but also finds that guest value varies across program tiers and that amenity returns vary by amenity and brand.

For this project, the defensible conclusion is: **VIP recognition or personalization may support service and retention, but profitability must be measured for the specific benefit and property.** A status label or high guest spend does not by itself show that an expensive benefit is profitable.

## What counts as a VIP-service cost?

| Cost category | Examples | Evidence and interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brand loyalty-program fees | Assessments paid by a hotel owner to participate in the chain program | CBRE found these averaged 2.2% of rooms revenue in its 2024 sample of 4,187 U.S. hotels. This is a program fee, not the full cost of serving a VIP guest. |
| Member benefits delivered at the property | Complimentary breakfast or lounge F&B, upgrades, extra housekeeping/service, points or other service recovery | CBRE identifies these costs separately from program fees. Their cost depends on what is actually used and the property's marginal cost and capacity. |
| Lounge operations | Food, beverage, supplies, and staff time for an executive lounge | USALI 12 adds more detail for executive-lounge reporting and distinguishes loyalty-member benefit costs. This supports tracking costs separately; it does not establish that lounge service is profitable. |
| Staff time and service delivery | Finding the profile, preparing a preference, making a handover, or responding to a guest | Public studies do not provide a representative cost-per-VIP estimate for this work. A property would need to measure staff minutes and any resulting service or revenue outcome. |

USALI 12 took effect on **January 1, 2026**. Its revised reporting categories make loyalty benefits and executive-lounge operations more visible in hotel accounts. The standard is useful for designing a measurement approach, but is not itself evidence of positive ROI. [AHLA/HFTP announcement](https://www.ahla.com/news/hftp-ahla-and-gfc-unveil-groundbreaking-12th-revised-edition-uniform-system-accounts-lodging) · [HFTP USALI 12 Rooms schedule](https://www.hftp.org/downloads/documents/usali/usali12_060624_rooms.pdf)

## What evidence says about financial returns

### Loyalty programs can contribute to hotel performance overall

Hua et al. analyzed **2,120 individual hotel properties from 2011 to 2013**, using cross-sectional and panel analyses and an instrumental-variable approach. They found loyalty-program expenses had a positive effect on RevPAR, ADR, occupancy, and gross operating profit (GOP), controlling for factors such as franchise, e-commerce, advertising, other marketing expense, hotel size, and chain scale. This is evidence about **overall program spending at hotel level**; it does not estimate the return on a specific VIP perk, lounge visit, or staff-recognition action. The data are also historical and mostly describe the U.S. hotel environment. [Hua et al. (2018), article record and abstract](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/9162) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/IJCHM-12-2016-0643)

CBRE reports that loyalty members contributed **52.8% of occupied rooms** in 2024 across five major U.S. hotel brand families, and that loyalty programs delivered 12% more room nights than in 2023. However, average room nights per member declined from 1.1 to 1.0. These are measures of member participation and room-night contribution, not proof that all those nights were incremental or profitable after fees and benefits. [CBRE, “For Hotel Owners Loyalty Program Benefits and Costs Continue to Rise”](https://www.cbre.com/insights/articles/for-hotel-owners-loyalty-program-benefits-and-costs-continue-to-rise)

### Loyalty tier is not the same as guest profitability

Rita, Borges-Tiago, and Caetano analyzed **498,655 guest profiles** from one multinational hotel chain. Their data-driven customer segments did not align well with conventional loyalty tiers, and some high-tier members generated less revenue than some low-tier members. The study measures revenue and segmentation, not profit after the costs of benefits. It cautions against treating status alone as a reliable reason to allocate costly perks. [Rita et al. (2023), accepted manuscript](https://research.unl.pt/entities/publication/90788fa3-56cf-491d-b704-69e9c790dbe8) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/IJCHM-05-2022-0646)

### Returns differ by perk and hotel brand

Hamilton et al. developed an ROI model for hotel amenities using guest-choice research and customer records from a multibrand global hotel company. Returns varied across amenities and across brands for the same amenity; the researchers also checked one amenity against a controlled before-and-after implementation. Their method counts both initial hotel choice and repeat purchase. Although this study was not specifically about VIP benefits, it supports evaluating each perk with local cost and guest-behavior data instead of assuming that a benefit pays for itself. [Hamilton et al. (2017), *Journal of Marketing Research*](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.14.0364)

## How to estimate whether a VIP benefit pays off

For a pilot, use **incremental GOP contribution**, not revenue alone:

> Incremental contribution per treated stay or lounge visit = attributable incremental room, F&B, or other contribution + expected contribution from additional repeat/direct bookings − loyalty-program fee allocated to the stay − actual cost of benefits used − added staff/service-recovery cost − opportunity cost of displaced paid upgrades or limited lounge capacity.

This is a measurement framework, not a published industry benchmark. Compare treated cases with a reasonable baseline, record whether the benefit was actually used, and avoid crediting a program for room nights or spending that would have happened anyway. If testing a guest-context tool, separately measure staff time to retrieve information, whether the information changed the service, guest response, and any attributable margin outcome.

## Implication for the hotel projec

The research supports a careful business case: hotels incur visible loyalty fees and property-level benefit costs, while program-level studies indicate possible gains in GOP and demand. It does **not** show that a specific VIP gesture is profitable, or that business travelers will choose a hotel because staff recognize them. For the lounge/F&B hypothesis, first identify one service moment and one benefit or staff action. Then measure its real delivery cost and whether it changes guest outcomes or incremental contribution. Keep the smart-glasses concept separate from the financial hypothesis until that workflow is validated.

## Evidence limits

- The 2.2% fee figure comes from U.S. hotels in one year and should not be applied directly to a Singapore property.
- Loyalty-member room share is not the same as incremental demand, and revenue is not profit.
- The 2018 hotel-level study concerns loyalty-program expenses as a whole, not the economics of individual perks.
- The tier-value study covers one chain and measures revenue, not net contribution after servicing costs.
- The amenity study shows why ROI can vary; its results do not establish the ROI of lounge access, VIP recognition, or personalization.
- I did not find a representative published estimate of net profit per VIP stay, per lounge visit, or per individual VIP benefit.
