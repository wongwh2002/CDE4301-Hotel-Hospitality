# What hotel guests value in personalization

**Research date:** 2026-10-09
**Purpose:** Identify which types of hotel personalization have the clearest guest-side evidence, with attention to frontline lounge and F&B service.

## Short assessmen

There is no well-supported universal ranking of the “most important” kind of hotel personalization. The evidence points to different things for different guests and moments. Broad hotel survey results favor **service that remembers the situation and responds well**: having stay details ready, following up on a problem, giving the guest choices, and asking for feedback. For F&B guests with dietary restrictions, **being able to modify a menu item** is a particularly valued inclusion cue. Qualitative interviews with frequent luxury guests also find value in staff recognizing and remembering them, but that result should not be generalized to all travelers.

For the lounge/F&B project, a useful hypothesis is:

> Guests are more likely to value context when it helps staff respond to a current need or preference in time. Recognition can help staff reach that context, but a name or identity match alone does not show that the service became more relevant.

This is a synthesis, not a validated finding about the Novotel or PARKROYAL workflows.

## What the evidence says

### 1. Service continuity and guest control show up strongly in a broad hotel survey

Medallia Market Research surveyed **1,749 U.S. hotel guests** in November 2023 about a recent stay. The sample came from Medallia’s CheckMarket/Sense360 panel and was weighted by U.S. age and gender. Among the **most noticed** of 52 possible personalization elements were: an opportunity to give service feedback (66%), follow-up on an issue (66%), choice in how an issue was resolved (65%), staff already having stay details at the start of an interaction (65%), and choice of support channel (64%). These are recall results, not a direct ranking of importance.

When asked what hotels should do better, guests selected their top five from 16 options. The leading wishes were rewards based on customer status/history (26%), more room/stay customization (24%), benefits or dedicated service for especially valuable guests (23%), being asked for feedback (22%), and choosing how to be served when an issue arises (21%). These are stated preferences, not observed choices or evidence of increased revenue.

One useful counterpoint: incorporating allergies or dietary preferences at hotel restaurants was recalled by **43%**, placing it among the report’s bottom five noticed elements. A broad sample may not reveal the value of a feature to the smaller group who need it; the dietary-specific research below addresses that group directly. The same report found a name on a welcome message displayed on the room TV was recalled by 42%, also among the bottom five. That does not measure the effect of a natural in-person greeting, but it cautions against treating visible name use as sufficient personalization.

Source: [Medallia, *Understanding Personalization Efforts in the Hospitality and Retail Industries*](https://www.medallia.com/wp-content/uploads/pdf/resources/Medallia-Understanding-Personalization-Efforts-in-the-Hospitality-and-Retail-Industries-Report.pdf). Vendor-produced U.S. survey; figures describe recall and stated wishes.

### 2. Menu flexibility matters to diners with dietary restrictions

Norris et al. (2026) used interviews, conjoint ranking, and an empirical test to study how restaurant menus signal inclusion to people with dietary restrictions. In the conjoint ranking, **availability of menu modifications** was the most valued inclusive cue. The tested inclusion cues also affected diners’ emotional and attitudinal responses and their behavioral intentions. This is the closest evidence for a concrete F&B personalization type, but it applies to guests with dietary restrictions, not hotel guests as a whole. The publisher’s accessible abstract does not report sample sizes or geography.

Source: Norris et al., “A seat at the table: Designing menus that signal inclusion,” *International Journal of Hospitality Management*, 133 (2026), 104425. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhm.2025.104425) · [Publisher abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0278431925003536).

### 3. Tailoring can feel personal, but the strongest gesture is not settled

Lei et al. (2024) surveyed **214 respondents in China** about ten hotel practices and asked how personalized each felt on a seven-point scale. Scores were close: a personal butler scored 5.13, special-occasion room decorations 5.11, a birthday special 5.07, tailored travel recommendations 5.02, anticipating needs from previous records 4.96, and greeting guests by name 4.94. These are ratings of perceived personalization—not a priority ranking, actual behavior, or lounge/F&B evidence. The study’s broader experiment with 919 participants found that effort and surprise can shape whether a hotel service is perceived as personalized.

Source: Lei, Wang, Fong, and Ye, “Recipe for perceived personalization in hotels,” *Tourism Management*, 100 (2024), 104818. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tourman.2023.104818) · [Publisher page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261517723001000).

### 4. Recognition may matter in frequent luxury travel

Buehring and O’Mahony interviewed **25 frequent luxury-hotel guests**. A prominent guest-side theme was being acknowledged and remembered as an individual; participants also valued staff knowledge and human interaction. This supports considering recognition in a VIP or luxury lounge, but the purposive, small luxury-traveler sample does not show that recognition is a priority for average hotel guests or price-led travelers.

Source: Buehring and O’Mahony (2019), “Designing memorable guest experiences: Development of constructs and value generating factors in luxury hotels,” *Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Insights*, 2(4), 358–376. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1108/JHTI-11-2018-0077) · [Open manuscript](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/92274/1/JHTI_Memorable_Experiences_Buehring_OMahony_Apr_2019.pdf).

## Implications for the projec

- **Start with usable context:** Prior requests, a current service issue, or a preference relevant to the present interaction may help staff avoid making a guest repeat themselves and choose an appropriate response.
- **Treat preference fit as guest-specific:** Dietary modifications have strong evidence for diners who need them; do not assume they matter equally to every guest.
- **Keep guest choice in the loop:** Survey respondents valued control over how issues were resolved and how they contacted the hotel. Personalization should not mean forcing an assumed preference on the guest.
- **Measure recognition, action, and perceived fit separately:** A returning guest can be recognized without staff retrieving or using relevant context. A profile can be shown without changing the service. Record what staff knew, what they did, and whether the guest noticed or valued the adaptation.
- **Do not frame the evidence as proof for smart glasses or face matching:** The studies support examining how relevant guest context reaches frontline staff. They do not establish that identity matching or any particular interface is needed.
